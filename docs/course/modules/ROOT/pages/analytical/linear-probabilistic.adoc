How can a fitted linear rule predict a measurement, and how can we check its errors on new observations? Start with the model and its least-squares algorithm, then evaluate the fixed rule on later data. The probability section develops an optional interpretation of squared errors and fitted coefficients.

== Mathematical setting

Let stem:[X\in\mathbb R^{n\times p}] be the design matrix, stem:[y\in\mathbb R^n] the response, and stem:[\beta\in\mathbb R^p] the coefficient vector.

.Definition: Ordinary least-squares estimator
[.def#def:ols]
****
The ordinary least-squares estimator minimises the empirical squared loss

[stem]
++++
\widehat\beta
=\operatorname*{argmin}_{\beta\in\mathbb R^p}
\frac{1}{n}\lVert y-X\beta\rVert_2^2.
++++
****

Let stem:[x_i^\top] be row stem:[i] of stem:[X]. It contains the
predictor values for observation stem:[i], including a leading 1 when we fit
an intercept. For a trial vector stem:[\beta], the prediction is
stem:[x_i^\top\beta] and the residual is stem:[e_i(\beta)=y_i-x_i^\top\beta].
Write stem:[J(\beta)=\sum_{i=1}^n e_i(\beta)^2] for the sum of squared
residuals. The factor stem:[1/n] in the definition changes the scale of the
objective and leaves its minimizers unchanged.

[%collapsible]
.Proof: differentiate one coefficient at a time
====
The entry stem:[X_{ij}] is the rate of change of prediction stem:[i]
when coefficient stem:[\beta_j] changes. The corresponding derivative of
the residual is stem:[-X_{ij}]. The chain rule gives

[stem]
++++
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\partial J}{\partial\beta_j}
  &= \sum_{i=1}^n 2e_i(\beta)(-X_{ij}),\\
\nabla_\beta J(\beta)
  &= -2X^\top e(\beta)=2X^\top(X\beta-y).
\end{aligned}
++++

The entries of the gradient are the stem:[p] partial derivatives. Setting
each to zero gives

[stem]
++++
\begin{aligned}
X^\top (y-X\widehat\beta)&=0,\\
X^\top X\widehat\beta&=X^\top y.
\end{aligned}
++++

These are two forms of the *normal equations*. The first says that the
residual is perpendicular to every column of stem:[X]. For example, an
intercept column of ones makes the residuals sum to zero on the training
rows. For any other coefficient vector stem:[\gamma], the difference
stem:[X(\widehat\beta-\gamma)] lies in the column space and is perpendicular
to the fitted residual. Expanding the squared norm gives

[stem]
++++
\lVert y-X\gamma\rVert_2^2
  = \lVert y-X\widehat\beta\rVert_2^2
    + \lVert X(\widehat\beta-\gamma)\rVert_2^2.
++++

The second term is nonnegative, which proves that a solution of the normal
equations minimizes the error. It vanishes only when the predictions
agree. If the columns are independent, it vanishes only for
stem:[\gamma=\widehat\beta].
====

[#_numerical_contract]
== Least-squares algorithm

*What do we compute?* From a training design stem:[X] and its measured
responses stem:[y], compute coefficients stem:[\widehat\beta], fitted
values stem:[X\widehat\beta], and residuals stem:[y-X\widehat\beta].
Use a separate design with the same columns to estimate responses on
new rows.

. Build the design in a fixed column order. Fit any centering and scaling
  using the training window. Add a column of ones if the model has an intercept.
. Call `beta, _, rank, singular = np.linalg.lstsq(X, y, rcond=None)`.
  Here `rank` counts singular values above the solver's threshold,
  and `singular` lists the singular values. Compute the fitted values and
  residuals with `X @ beta` and `y - X @ beta`.
. Check `X.T @ residual` is close to zero on the training rows, up to
  numerical precision. If the rank is smaller than the number of columns,
  several coefficient vectors can yield the same fitted values. Check for
  duplicate predictors or linear combinations of existing predictors.

=== How the SVD gives the algorithm

For stem:[n\ge p], write the reduced SVD as
stem:[X=U\Sigma V^\top]. The columns stem:[u_j] of stem:[U] are
orthonormal directions in the space of fitted responses, the columns
stem:[v_j] of stem:[V] are orthonormal directions for the coefficients,
and stem:[\Sigma] has the singular values stem:[\sigma_j] on its diagonal.
Each pair satisfies stem:[Xv_j=\sigma_j u_j]. In PCA we previously used
the right singular vectors as directions in the variable space. Here
they specify combinations of coefficients.

. Compute stem:[c_j=u_j^\top y], the coordinates of the observed response
  along the columns of stem:[U].
. In coefficient coordinates stem:[z=V^\top\beta], prediction
  stem:[X\beta=U\Sigma z] has coordinate stem:[\sigma_j z_j] along
  stem:[u_j]. The part of stem:[y] perpendicular to these directions is
  the same for every fit. By orthogonality, minimize each squared difference
  stem:[(c_j-\sigma_j z_j)^2] separately.
. For each positive stem:[\sigma_j], take stem:[z_j=c_j/\sigma_j].
  For a zero singular value, take stem:[z_j=0] to get the
  smallest Euclidean norm among all minimizing coefficient vectors.
  In floating-point arithmetic the solver treats sufficiently small
  singular values as zero. This leaves a numerical rank that depends
  on the tolerance.
. Return to the original coefficient coordinates with
  stem:[\widehat\beta=Vz]. The notation stem:[\Sigma^+] denotes
  the diagonal operation that divides by retained singular values
  and leaves all other coordinates at zero, so
  stem:[\widehat\beta=V\Sigma^+U^\top y].

A small nonzero singular value means that a large change in a particular
combination of coefficients makes only a small change in predictions.
For example, a change of stem:[0.01] in stem:[c_j] changes stem:[z_j]
by stem:[10] when stem:[\sigma_j=0.001]. If both predictors are nearly
copies of one another, increasing one coefficient and decreasing the
other can leave the predictions almost unchanged.

[%collapsible]
.Why solve using X directly?
====
For full column rank, define the 2-norm condition number of stem:[X] as
stem:[\kappa_2(X)=\sigma_{\max}/\sigma_{\min}], the ratio of its largest
to its smallest singular value. The eigenvalues of
stem:[X^\top X=V\Sigma^2V^\top] are stem:[\sigma_j^2], so
stem:[\kappa_2(X^\top X)=\kappa_2(X)^2].
For example, a ratio of stem:[10^5] becomes stem:[10^{10}] in the normal
matrix. Solvers based on SVD or on QR factorization work directly with
stem:[X] and avoid this extra amplification. Tiny singular values of
stem:[X] still limit how precisely the data determine the coefficients.
====

[#_error_estimation]
== Error estimation on later data

*Question for the data.* How far are the estimates from the measurements on
the period of interest? Once the rule is fitted, use it to make predictions
stem:[\widehat y_i] on stem:[m] held-out rows. A *held-out* row belongs
to a period set aside for evaluation. It contributes to the error
calculation after the parameters and the preprocessing have been fixed.

. For each evaluated row, compute stem:[e_i=y_i-\widehat y_i] in the
  units of the response. A positive value means the estimate was too low.
. Square each difference to get a loss stem:[\ell_i=e_i^2].
  Average these losses to estimate the mean squared prediction error,
  stem:[\widehat R=m^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^m\ell_i]. Take the square root to
  obtain RMSE in the response units. You can also report MAE, the
  average of stem:[|e_i|].
. Compare models on the same rows. Find a station-hour with a large
  error and read its estimate and measurement. Then examine whether
  nearby station-hours show similar errors.

For example, measured values of 30 and 60 and estimates of 25 and 70 give
residuals +5 and −10. MAE is 7.5, and RMSE is
stem:[\sqrt{(25+100)/2}\approx7.9]. This summarizes two prediction errors
in the measurement units. The first estimate is too low and the second
is too high.

[%collapsible]
.How uncertain is the mean test loss?
====
Suppose the fitted rule is fixed and the stem:[m] test units are independent
draws from the same evaluation population with finite loss variance. The
sample standard deviation stem:[s_\ell] measures the spread of the individual
squared losses. The estimated standard error stem:[s_\ell/\sqrt m] measures
how much their average would fluctuate across repeated test samples. This
comes from adding the variances of independent losses and dividing by
stem:[m^2].

Nearby Beijing hours may share a pollution episode, including across
stations. Positive dependence means that many rows can carry repeated
information. With enough data, repeat the evaluation over several
chronological windows. Another method is a *block bootstrap*, which
resamples chunks of consecutive hours together to preserve some of the
dependence within each chunk.
====

[#_probability_connection]
== Why Gaussian errors lead to least squares

The least-squares algorithm and the validation steps above are sufficient
for the 23 November practical. The following extension asks a different
question: under a specified probability model, why does the same squared
error arise?

=== Start with a likelihood you can compute by hand

Suppose two independent binary observations are success and failure,
in that order. For a candidate success probability stem:[\theta] the
chance of getting that sequence is stem:[\theta(1-\theta)].
For stem:[\theta=0.5] it is stem:[0.25], and for stem:[\theta=0.9] it
is stem:[0.09]. The *likelihood* is this expression viewed as a
function of the candidate parameter, with the observations already fixed.
Maximizing it chooses the parameter for which the observed sequence was
most probable. Taking the natural logarithm preserves the order and turns
the product into the sum stem:[\log\theta+\log(1-\theta)].

A continuous observation uses a probability *density*. A density describes
probability per unit of measurement. At a time and station with a fixed
set of predictors, the density at a measured value describes how much
probability the model puts in a very small interval around that value,
per unit of interval width. To compare parameters, evaluate the joint
density at the same measured values each time.

=== Identify the data, parameters and assumptions

Treat the predictor matrix stem:[X] as fixed. Capital stem:[Y_i]
represents the random response before measurement, and lowercase stem:[y_i]
is its observed value. Assume stem:[Y_i=x_i^\top\beta+\varepsilon_i].
Here stem:[\beta] is the model's unknown coefficient vector, and
stem:[\varepsilon_i] is the random difference from the model mean
stem:[x_i^\top\beta]. Assume the differences are independent and have
the same normal distribution with mean zero and variance
stem:[\sigma^2>0], written stem:[\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2)].
For a candidate stem:[\beta], the observed difference is
stem:[y_i-x_i^\top\beta]. Its density is highest at zero and decreases
as the absolute difference grows.

[%collapsible]
.Derivation: from the Gaussian density to squared errors
====
For one row, the density stem:[f_i] is

[stem]
++++
f_i(y_i\mid x_i,\beta,\sigma^2)=
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}}\,
\exp\!\left(-\frac{(y_i-x_i^\top\beta)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right).
++++

Independence makes the joint density the product of the row densities.
With stem:[X] and stem:[y] fixed, that product is the likelihood
stem:[L(\beta,\sigma^2)]. Taking the logarithm turns the product into
a sum and cancels each exponential. Use a minus sign to express the
maximization as a minimization:

[stem]
++++
\begin{aligned}
-\log L(\beta,\sigma^2)
  &= \sum_{i=1}^{n} -\log f_i(y_i\mid x_i,\beta,\sigma^2) \\
  &= \sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[
     \tfrac12\log(2\pi\sigma^2)
     +\frac{(y_i-x_i^\top\beta)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right]\\
  &=\tfrac n2\log(2\pi\sigma^2)
     +\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}J(\beta).
\end{aligned}
++++

For fixed stem:[\sigma^2], the first term is constant in stem:[\beta]
and the factor of stem:[J(\beta)] is positive. Thus both objectives
select the same coefficients. The reasoning uses the normal density
to get a square, independence to multiply densities, and the common
variance to give every square equal weight.
====

=== Estimate noise and coefficient uncertainty under the model

The residual sum of squares stem:[J(\widehat\beta)] measures how much
variation is left in the training responses after fitting. If the
stem:[p] columns of stem:[X] are independent and stem:[n>p], fitting
uses stem:[p] directions in response space. There are stem:[n-p]
orthogonal directions left. Under the independent, constant-variance
error model, each remaining unit direction has noise variance
stem:[\sigma^2]. This gives the unbiased residual-based estimator
stem:[\widehat\sigma^2=J(\widehat\beta)/(n-p)].
Estimating the error level after fitting here plays the same role as using
stem:[n-1] after fitting just a mean.

[%collapsible]
.Why the coefficient estimates vary across repeated samples
====
Before measuring stem:[Y], the estimate stem:[\widehat\beta] is a random
vector. With full column rank, the normal equations and
stem:[Y=X\beta+\varepsilon] yield

[stem]
++++
\widehat\beta=\beta+(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top\varepsilon.
++++

This equation describes the effect of the noise on the coefficients.
With stem:[\operatorname{Cov}(\varepsilon\mid X)=\sigma^2 I_n],
where stem:[I_n] is the identity matrix, propagating covariance through
the linear map stem:[B=(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top] yields

[stem]
++++
\operatorname{Cov}(\widehat\beta\mid X)
  =\sigma^2 BB^\top
  =\sigma^2(X^\top X)^{-1}.
++++

The diagonal entries are the sampling variances of the fitted
coefficients under the model. Using stem:[\widehat\sigma^2] gives
estimated variances, and their square roots give standard errors.
Near-dependent predictors make some estimated directions highly
variable. On the Beijing extract, dependence across hours and stations
also calls for chronological evaluation. Use the holdout error above
to describe observed prediction accuracy.
====

== Worked example: exact fit versus coefficient stability

[.worked-example-context#worked-linear-context]
=== Context

*Importance:* Essential (P0)

Start with an exact line, then introduce two nearly collinear predictors. Compare three quantities: residual error on the training rows, coefficient sensitivity, and error on future data.

[.worked-example-method#worked-linear-method]
=== Method

*Importance:* Core (P1)

.Example: Exact line and residual error
[.examp#ex:exact-line]
****
For stem:[x=(0,1,2)] and stem:[y=(1,3,5)], use

[stem]
++++
X=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\1&1\\1&2\end{bmatrix}.
++++

The vector stem:[\widehat\beta=(1,2)^\top] gives stem:[X\widehat\beta=y], so the residual norm and training RMSE are zero. This does not imply zero future error: three noiseless collinear points tell us little about curvature, changing noise, or a new population. If the last response becomes stem:[5.3], the fitted coefficient and residual uncertainty must be recomputed rather than treating stem:[(1,2)] as exact truth.
****

.Mathematical insight — conditioning controls coefficient sensitivity
[IMPORTANT]
====
[.notebook-insight-label]
*Mathematical insight.*

*Core (P1).* If stem:[X=U\Sigma V^\top], least squares divides data components by singular values. A small stem:[\sigma_{\min}] amplifies perturbations, and forming stem:[X^\top X] squares the 2-norm condition number: stem:[\kappa_2(X^\top X)=\kappa_2(X)^2]. Zero residual error therefore says nothing about coefficient stability. SVD-based least squares avoids the squared-conditioning computation but cannot create information in a weakly identified direction.
====

[.worked-example-implementation#worked-linear-implementation]
=== Implementation

*Importance:* Essential (P0)

The hand calculation is exact because the small design identifies its two coefficients. The executable example makes two predictor columns nearly identical. It compares solving the normal equations directly with `numpy.linalg.lstsq` and Ridge, a method that penalizes large coefficients. Read the training RMSE beside the fitted coefficients. Do the methods with similar errors give similar coefficients?

[#linear-conditioning-table]
[%dynamic%open,python,fail-on-error=,output=table,table-caption="Stable and unstable fits for a nearly collinear design",table-description="Rows compare a direct solve of the normal equations, singular-value least squares, and Ridge. Columns report two slope coefficients, training RMSE, and the design condition number."]
----
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
from sklearn.linear_model import Ridge

# Fix the random generator so the webpage and notebook reproduce the same example.
rng = np.random.default_rng(20260722)
n = 250
x1 = rng.normal(size=n)
x2 = x1 + 1.0e-7 * rng.normal(size=n)
X = np.column_stack([np.ones(n), x1, x2])
y = 2.0 + 3.0 * x1 - 3.0 * x2 + 0.05 * rng.normal(size=n)


def rmse(beta: np.ndarray) -> float:
    """Compute training RMSE only for this numerical sensitivity experiment."""
    return float(np.sqrt(np.mean((y - X @ beta) ** 2)))


# The normal-equation solve is included as a diagnostic counterexample, not advice.
beta_normal = np.linalg.solve(X.T @ X, X.T @ y)
beta_lstsq, _, rank, singular_values = np.linalg.lstsq(X, y, rcond=None)
ridge = Ridge(alpha=1.0e-3, fit_intercept=False).fit(X, y)
condition = float(np.linalg.cond(X))
assert rank == X.shape[1]
assert condition > 1.0e6

fit_summary = pd.DataFrame(
    {
        "method": ["normal equations", "SVD least squares", "Ridge α=10⁻³"],
        "coefficient x1": [beta_normal[1], beta_lstsq[1], ridge.coef_[1]],
        "coefficient x2": [beta_normal[2], beta_lstsq[2], ridge.coef_[2]],
        "training RMSE": [rmse(beta_normal), rmse(beta_lstsq), rmse(ridge.coef_)],
        "κ₂(X)": [condition, condition, condition],
    }
).set_index("method")
fit_summary
----

Nearly cancelling coefficients are poorly identified even though predictions and RMSE can remain stable. Ridge changes the estimand by adding shrinkage; it is not merely a numerically clever way to obtain the unregularised coefficients.

[.worked-example-conclusion#worked-linear-conclusion]
=== Conclusion

*Importance:* Essential (P0)

An exact or low-RMSE fit does not establish stable coefficients or future predictive validity. RMSE checks agreement on observed responses, the residual plot challenges mean and variance structure, and the singular values diagnose coefficient sensitivity. Ridge changes the estimand through shrinkage rather than recovering the unstable unregularised coefficients. None of these diagnostics estimates performance on a new population; the generator fixes one noise law and one collinearity mechanism, so its conclusions describe this numerical experiment.
