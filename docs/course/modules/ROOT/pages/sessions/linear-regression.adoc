[#nov23-reconstruction]
== 1. Reconstruct the six measured variables

Let stem:[A] be the table with one line per retained station-hour and one
column per measured variable. Write stem:[n] for the number of rows,
stem:[p=6] for the number of variables, and stem:[\bar a_j] and stem:[s_j]
for the mean and sample standard deviation of column stem:[j]. The
standardized table has entries stem:[(A_z)_{ij}=(A_{ij}-\bar a_j)/s_j].
The columns of stem:[V_k] are the first stem:[k] unit principal directions,
ordered by decreasing variance. Each direction has one coefficient per
variable. In the notebook, take the first stem:[k] rows of `Vt` from the
SVD of stem:[A_z] and transpose them to form stem:[V_k].

[cols="2,2,2,4",options="header"]
|===
|Step |Calculation |Shape |What each row contains

|Project |stem:[Z_k=A_zV_k] |stem:[n\times k] |Scores on the chosen axes
|Reconstruct |stem:[\widehat A_z=Z_kV_k^\top] |stem:[n\times p] |Approximate values on the original variable axes, still standardized
|Restore units |stem:[\widehat A_{ij}=\bar a_j+s_j(\widehat A_z)_{ij}] |stem:[n\times p] |Approximate measurements in physical units
|===

For example, an approximate standardized temperature of 1.5 corresponds to
13 °C if the original temperature mean and standard deviation are 10 °C and
2 °C. With two directions, every reconstructed row combines two scores with
two directions. Each direction supplies a coefficient for all six variables.

[%collapsible]
.Why does the reconstruction error decrease?
====
The residual table stem:[A_z-\widehat A_z] contains the projections on the
omitted orthogonal axes. Each omitted score column has squared norm
stem:[(n-1)\lambda_j], where stem:[\lambda_j] is its sample variance. By
orthogonality, adding a component removes precisely its squared score norm
from the sum of squared reconstruction errors. Thus, when total variance is
positive, the error relative to a reconstruction using only the means is

[stem]
++++
\frac{\lVert A_z-\widehat A_z\rVert_F^2}{\lVert A_z\rVert_F^2}
=
\frac{\sum_{j=k+1}^p\lambda_j}{\sum_{j=1}^p\lambda_j}.
++++

The Frobenius norm squared sums the squared differences of all cells. The
equality uses standardized coordinates throughout.
====

Discuss why two score columns reconstruct six variable columns. Compare the
relative errors for k=2 and k=3. The
xref:sessions/matrix-geometry.adoc[2 November lesson] derives the PCA directions
and supplies a worked calculation.

[#nov23-simple]
== 2. Fit a line to a designated response

If PM2.5 is unavailable at a given station-hour, estimate it from PM10 and,
later, from meteorological values measured at that same station-hour. For
observed pairs stem:[(x_i,y_i)], take x to be PM10 and y to be PM2.5. Let
stem:[e_i=y_i-(\beta_0+\beta_1x_i)] be the observed value minus the fitted
value. Ordinary least squares minimizes the sum of the squared residuals.

[%collapsible]
.From the squared error to the slope and intercept
====
Write stem:[m] for the number of training pairs and stem:[J] for
their sum of squared residuals. The derivative with respect to the intercept
gives the first equation below. The second follows by summing the three terms
and dividing by stem:[m].

[stem]
++++
\begin{aligned}
0 &= -2\sum_{i=1}^m (y_i-\widehat\beta_0-\widehat\beta_1x_i),\\
\widehat\beta_0 &= \bar y-\widehat\beta_1\bar x.
\end{aligned}
++++

For each candidate slope, inserting this intercept formula changes the
residual to stem:[(y_i-\bar y)-\beta_1(x_i-\bar x)]. Differentiating the
sum of the squares of these centered residuals with respect to the slope,
then solving for it, gives

[stem]
++++
\begin{aligned}
0 &= -2\sum_{i=1}^m(x_i-\bar x)
       \big[(y_i-\bar y)-\widehat\beta_1(x_i-\bar x)\big],\\
\widehat\beta_1 &=
\frac{\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)(y_i-\bar y)}{\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)^2}
=\frac{s_{xy}}{s_x^2}.
\end{aligned}
++++

Here stem:[s_{xy}] is the training sample covariance and stem:[s_x^2] the
training sample variance of PM10, computed on the same pairs. If the response
also varies, the slope is stem:[r_{xy}s_y/s_x], where stem:[r_{xy}] is Pearson
correlation and stem:[s_x,s_y] are the sample standard deviations. A slope
has units of response per unit of predictor.
====

[#nov23-multiple]
== 3. Fit several predictors

Write stem:[m] for the number of training rows and stem:[d] for the number
of predictors. The *design matrix* stem:[X] has shape stem:[m\times(d+1)].
Its first column contains ones, which multiply the intercept. The remaining
columns contain predictor values in a fixed order. The vector stem:[\beta]
has stem:[d+1] entries, one coefficient per column. Multiplying stem:[X\beta]
produces one estimate per row, in the same order as the measurements in
stem:[y]. For example, a row stem:[(1,2,-1)] gives a prediction of
stem:[\beta_0+2\beta_1-\beta_2].

Define the trial residual vector stem:[e(\beta)=y-X\beta] and the squared
error stem:[J(\beta)=\lVert e(\beta)\rVert_2^2]. A coefficient vector
stem:[\widehat\beta] that minimizes stem:[J] satisfies the normal equations.

[stem]
++++
\begin{aligned}
X^\top X\widehat\beta &= X^\top y,\\
X^\top(y-X\widehat\beta) &= 0.
\end{aligned}
++++

[%collapsible]
.From the squared error to the normal equations
====
Write stem:[X_{ij}] for the entry in row stem:[i] and column stem:[j].
For this calculation, index the columns from stem:[0] to stem:[d].
Increasing coefficient stem:[\beta_j] by a small amount changes the estimate
in row stem:[i] at rate stem:[X_{ij}] and changes its residual at rate
stem:[-X_{ij}]. The chain rule for the square therefore gives

[stem]
++++
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\partial J}{\partial\beta_j}
  &= \sum_{i=1}^m 2 e_i(\beta)(-X_{ij}),\\
\nabla_\beta J(\beta) &= -2X^\top e(\beta)=2X^\top(X\beta-y).
\end{aligned}
++++

The gradient puts one derivative per coefficient into a vector. Setting it
to zero yields the two equivalent equations above. Each component of
stem:[X^\top e(\widehat\beta)] is the dot product of a design column with
the fitted residual. In particular, the column of ones shows that the
training residuals sum to zero.

For any other coefficient vector, the change in predictions is a
combination of the design columns. It is perpendicular to the fitted
residual. By Pythagoras, the new squared error is the fitted squared error
plus the squared norm of that change. This establishes the minimum.
====

Compute the coefficients with `np.linalg.lstsq(Xtr, ytr, rcond=None)`, where
`Xtr` and `ytr` are the training design and response. Multiply `Xva @ beta`
to predict, using a validation design with the same columns and the same
training transformations. The fitted vector is unique. The coefficients
are unique when the design columns are linearly independent. The
xref:analytical/linear-probabilistic.adoc[linear regression reference]
develops the SVD algorithm, numerical sensitivity and prediction error. Its
probability section is optional for this session.

[#nov23-validation]
== 4. Compare models on a later window

Use March 1–10, 2013 for training and March 11–14 for validation. The same date
boundary applies across the three stations. On the common complete rows,
compare the training-mean rule, the PM10-only line, and a model using PM10,
temperature, pressure, dew point and wind speed. Compute any scaling
parameters on training rows and apply them to both windows.

For the same stem:[b] validation rows, subtract each prediction from its
measurement and call the difference stem:[e_i]. Compute the mean absolute
error (MAE) and root mean squared error (RMSE) over those differences.

[stem]
++++
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{MAE} &= \frac{1}{b}\sum_{i=1}^{b}|e_i|,\\
\operatorname{RMSE} &= \sqrt{\frac{1}{b}\sum_{i=1}^{b}e_i^2}.
\end{aligned}
++++

For two errors of 0 and 10 µg/m³ the MAE is 5 µg/m³ and the RMSE is about
7.1 µg/m³. Squaring gives more weight to larger errors. Both summaries are in
µg/m³. The residual at an individual station-hour gives the direction of the
error as well. When the measured value is 60 and the prediction is 45, the
residual is +15 µg/m³ and the model estimated too low by 15 µg/m³.

This experiment estimates PM2.5 *at the same hour* from other available
measurements. The later window supports a comparison in this short teaching
extract. To assess performance across periods, repeat the comparison over further chronological windows.
See xref:foundations/safe-pipelines.adoc[training and validation] and
xref:foundations/metrics-errors.adoc[regression error metrics] for references.

[cols="3,5",options="header"]
|===
|Question |Steps

|Can two or three PCA components reconstruct six measured variables?
|Fix the scale, project to the selected axes, reconstruct the table, then
 compare the errors for two and three components and restore physical units.

|Can we estimate missing PM2.5 at a station-hour from measurements available then?
|Choose the response and available predictors. Fit a training-mean reference,
 a PM10 line, and a PM10-and-weather model by least squares.

|Does adding weather improve the estimates on later dates?
|Keep one chronological split for all three rules. Apply the fixed rules to
 the same later rows and compare RMSE and MAE. Pick one large error, look up
 the measured and estimated PM2.5, and state its size and direction.
|===

*Takeaways.* Least squares gives the coefficients that minimize squared
training residuals. Adding predictors to a nested model can only decrease
or preserve that training error on the same rows. The comparison on later
dates checks how the fixed rules performed in this validation window.

*Assumptions and limits.* Each estimation uses predictors available at that
station-hour and the same column definitions as training. The evaluation
covers complete rows from three stations over four later days. The local
errors can be related across nearby hours and stations. A claim about other
periods needs further validation windows.

*Exit question:* Why does adding a predictor always weakly reduce the
training sum of squared errors for nested least-squares models on the same
rows? What does your later-window comparison actually show?
