== Recall: the Pearson matrix introduced on 20 October

Let n>1 be the number of common complete observations and p the number of
selected measured variables (*features*). A row is a point of feature space,
with p measured coordinates; a column contains n observations of one variable.
The matrices here contain *observed numbers*. Their uppercase names follow
matrix notation, rather than declaring random variables.

[cols="1,3",options="header"]
|===
|Notation |Meaning
|stem:[A\in\mathbb R^{n\times p}] |Observed data table; n rows and p variables.
|stem:[\bar a_j=n^{-1}\sum_i A_{ij}] |Mean of column j; stem:[\bar a] collects the p means.
|stem:[A_c=A-\mathbf1\bar a^\top] |Centered table; stem:[\mathbf1] contains n ones and stem:[\top] means transpose.
|stem:[S=A_c^\top A_c/(n-1)] |Sample covariance matrix on the same rows.
|stem:[s_j=\sqrt{S_{jj}}] |Positive sample standard deviation of column j.
|stem:[D=\operatorname{diag}(s_1^2,\ldots,s_p^2)] |Diagonal matrix of variances; zeros off the diagonal.
|stem:[A_z=A_cD^{-1/2}] |Centered columns divided by their standard deviations.
|stem:[R=A_z^\top A_z/(n-1)] |Pearson matrix, with diagonal one.
|===

For vectors u and v of the same length, stem:[u^\top v=\sum_i u_iv_i] is
the inner product and stem:[\lVert u\rVert_2=\sqrt{u^\top u}] is length.
A unit vector has length one; orthogonal vectors have inner product zero.
The matrix proofs, centering projector H, population-versus-observed
quantities and complete-row convention are in the
xref:./../sessions/joint-variation.adoc#thm:matrix-centering[20 October matrix introduction].

Our small example remains stem:[A=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&2\\3&4\\5&6\end{smallmatrix}\right)]:
its centered rows are (−2,−2), (0,0), (2,2), and
stem:[S=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}4&4\\4&4\end{smallmatrix}\right)].
The following cell reconstructs the common Beijing table so this notebook
runs independently of the previous notebook.

== 1. Why does PCA lead to eigenvectors?

=== From covariance and correlation to a new question

S and R describe relationships between pairs of original variables. We now
ask whether *combinations* of variables can summarize the cloud using fewer
coordinates. A direction stem:[v=(w_1,\ldots,w_p)^\top] has one weight
stem:[w_j] per original variable. On standardized values, the score of
observation i on this direction is

[stem]
++++
z_i=\sum_{j=1}^p (A_z)_{ij}w_j.
++++

This new coordinate need not be a physical sensor reading. It may describe
a common pattern or a contrast between several measurements; interpretation
requires reading its weights with the original variables. Here the input
columns and scores are dimensionless because the measurements were
standardized. A descriptive name for an axis is an interpretation to justify,
not an additional measurement or a causal mechanism.

The new mathematical tools answer specific questions: a projection gives
coordinates; variance measures retained spread; a unit-length constraint
prevents arbitrary rescaling; eigenvectors solve the maximization; SVD
computes the directions. PCA on S preserves spread in the chosen original
scales; PCA on R preserves spread after giving every variable unit variance.
In the formulas below use stem:[A_c,S] for covariance PCA, or substitute
stem:[A_z,R] for correlation PCA.

[cols="1,2,2",options="header"]
|===
|Choice |Table and matrix |What counts as a large variation?
|Covariance PCA |stem:[A_c] and stem:[S] |A large numerical departure in the chosen original scales.
|Correlation PCA (today's practical) |stem:[A_z] and stem:[R] |A departure measured in each variable's standard deviations.
|===

Standardization is a scientific choice. It prevents the mixed units in today's
six-variable table from determining the result, but also gives each variable
unit variance, including a noisy one. It does not make all measurements equally
reliable. The projection and eigenvector arguments are the same for both rows.

*Two-variable example.* The four pairs already studied have sample Pearson
0.8. After standardization their matrix is
stem:[R=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&0.8\\0.8&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)].
The unit directions stem:[(1,1)^\top/\sqrt2] and
stem:[(1,-1)^\top/\sqrt2] give a common-variation coordinate and a contrast.
Their score variances are 1.8 and 0.2; the first retains 90% of the total 2.
Neither direction is simply one of the original physical variables.

We want a direction preserving as much centered spread as possible. Project
the row cloud onto a unit feature direction stem:[v\in\mathbb R^p]. The
score vector is stem:[z=A_cv\in\mathbb R^n]. Each score is one observation’s coordinate along v. For example, choosing
v with first coordinate 1 and the rest zero selects the first centered
column; in our small table, the scores are (−2,0,2), of variance 4.
All these are *observed* scores, not a new random sample model. Their
empirical mean is zero, and their sample variance, denoted stem:[s_z^2], is

[stem]
++++
s_z^2=\frac{z^\top z}{n-1}=v^\top Sv.
++++

Recall the linear algebra vocabulary: a nonzero *eigenvector* stem:[v_j]
satisfies stem:[Sv_j=\lambda_jv_j]; stem:[\lambda_j] is its *eigenvalue*.
For symmetric S, the spectral theorem supplies an *orthonormal* eigenbasis:
each vector has length one, and distinct vectors have inner product zero.
Order eigenvalues stem:[\lambda_1\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_p\geq0].
A *principal direction* is a maximizing feature direction. The corresponding
*principal component scores* are its n projected coordinates.

.Proposition: PCA maximizes projected variance
[.thm#thm:matrix-pca]
****
The maximum of stem:[v^\top Sv] over unit v is stem:[\lambda_1], attained by a leading eigenvector. Scores stem:[z_j=A_cv_j] have variance stem:[\lambda_j] and are mutually uncorrelated.
****

[%collapsible]
.Proof — PCA maximizes projected variance
====
Without a norm constraint, scaling v makes this arbitrarily large. Therefore
the first principal direction solves
stem:[\max_{v^\top v=1}v^\top Sv].

*Step 1.* Define the objective stem:[f(v)=v^\top Sv] and write the constraint
as stem:[g(v)=v^\top v-1=0]. A *Lagrangian* combines them using an unknown
scalar multiplier stem:[\lambda]:

[stem]
++++
L(v,\lambda)=f(v)-\lambda g(v)=v^\top Sv-\lambda(v^\top v-1).
++++

The gradient is the vector of coordinate derivatives. Symmetry of S gives
stem:[\nabla_v f=2Sv], and stem:[\nabla_v g=2v]. At a constrained extremum
the objective cannot improve in a direction tangent to the unit sphere;
these gradients are parallel. The stationarity equations are

[stem]
++++
\nabla_vL=2Sv-2\lambda v=0,\qquad
\frac{\partial L}{\partial\lambda}=-(v^\top v-1)=0.
++++

The first equation gives stem:[Sv=\lambda v]; the second enforces unit
length. Multiplying the first by stem:[v^\top] gives stem:[v^\top Sv=\lambda]:
the multiplier is the projected variance at this candidate. We do not
maximize L jointly over v and the multiplier; these are stationary candidates.

*Step 2.* This gives stationary candidates; the spectral theorem proves the maximum: writing
stem:[v=\sum_j\alpha_jv_j], where stem:[\alpha_j=v_j^\top v] are its
coordinates in the orthonormal eigenbasis, and
stem:[\sum_j\alpha_j^2=1], gives
stem:[v^\top Sv=\sum_j\lambda_j\alpha_j^2\leq\lambda_1].

*Step 3.* Successive maxima under orthogonality constraints give the remaining
directions stem:[v_j]. The eigenvalue stem:[\lambda_j] is the variance of
score stem:[z_j=A_cv_j], and stem:[z_j^\top z_k/(n-1)=0] for distinct
orthogonal eigenvectors. Uncorrelated scores need not be independent.
The *explained variance ratio* is the fraction of total variance retained by
one component: stem:[\lambda_j/\sum_{\ell=1}^p\lambda_\ell], when
total variance is positive. Directions (p numbers) and scores (n numbers) are
different objects. Signs are arbitrary; repeated eigenvalues allow rotations
inside their eigenspaces.
====

Choose an integer stem:[1\leq k\leq p], the number of retained directions.
Let stem:[V_k=(v_1,\ldots,v_k)\in\mathbb R^{p\times k}] collect them as
columns and stem:[Z_k=A_cV_k\in\mathbb R^{n\times k}] collect the scores.
The *reconstruction*, denoted stem:[\widehat A], returns these k coordinates
to the original variables and restores the means:

[stem]
++++
\widehat A=\mathbf1\bar a^\top+Z_kV_k^\top.
++++

For example, with k=1, the reconstructed centered row i is its single score
multiplied by stem:[v_1^\top]. The *squared Frobenius error* sums the squared
errors of every table cell. For any matrix B, its Frobenius norm satisfies
stem:[\lVert B\rVert_F^2=\sum_{i,j}B_{ij}^2]; here use
stem:[B=A-\widehat A]. Orthogonal projection minimizes this error within
the retained subspace. Among centered approximations of rank at most k,
the leading principal directions minimize it globally. The SVD below makes
the omitted error explicit. This reconstruction objective uses no response
to predict, so it does not prove predictive relevance.

For today's *standardized* calculation, use stem:[Z_k=A_zV_k] and first
reconstruct stem:[\widehat A_z=Z_kV_k^\top]. This is an n-by-p table of
standardized values. To recover physical measurements, undo the scaling
column by column, then restore the means:

[stem]
++++
\widehat A_{ij}=\bar a_j+s_j(\widehat A_z)_{ij}.
++++

Measure the practical's reconstruction error in standardized coordinates,
so temperature, pressure and concentrations use the declared geometry.

== 2. Why SVD computes PCA

A *singular value decomposition* (SVD) factors a table into orthonormal
directions and nonnegative scales. The *economy* form keeps stem:[q=\min(n,p)]
columns, even if some scales are zero. Introduce its three matrices U,
stem:[\Sigma] and V with the dimensions below. Their columns, denoted
stem:[u_j] and stem:[v_j] for stem:[j=1,\ldots,q], satisfy
stem:[U^\top U=V^\top V=I_q]. Here stem:[I_q] is the identity of size q.
The diagonal matrix stem:[\Sigma=\operatorname{diag}(\sigma_1,\ldots,\sigma_q)]
contains the *singular values*, ordered stem:[\sigma_1\geq\cdots\geq\sigma_q\geq0].
The notation stem:[\sigma_j] denotes a singular value of a matrix, not the
feature sample standard deviation stem:[s_j] used above. Write the factorization:

[stem]
++++
A_c=U\Sigma V^\top,\quad
U\in\mathbb R^{n\times q},\quad\Sigma\in\mathbb R^{q\times q},\quad
V\in\mathbb R^{p\times q}.
++++

.Proposition: SVD yields PCA directions and variances
[.thm#thm:matrix-svd]
****
For this economy SVD, stem:[S=V\Sigma^\top\Sigma V^\top/(n-1)] and stem:[\lambda_j=\sigma_j^2/(n-1)] for stem:[j\leq q]. The associated scores are stem:[\sigma_ju_j].
****

[%collapsible]
.Proof — SVD yields PCA directions and variances
====
The columns of V may be chosen as the eigenvectors of S associated with
these singular values. We now verify both their directions and their variances.

*Step 1.* Transpose stem:[A_c=U\Sigma V^\top] to obtain stem:[A_c^\top=V\Sigma^\top U^\top].

*Step 2.* Multiply and use stem:[U^\top U=I_q]. This gives

[stem]
++++
S=V\frac{\Sigma^\top\Sigma}{n-1}V^\top,\qquad
\lambda_j=\frac{\sigma_j^2}{n-1},\quad
A_cv_j=\sigma_j u_j.
++++

*Step 3.* Multiply the SVD by stem:[v_j] and use stem:[V^\top v_j=e_j], the vector with 1 at coordinate j and 0 elsewhere, to obtain stem:[A_cv_j=\sigma_ju_j]. If stem:[q<p], the other stem:[p-q] eigenvalues of S are zero. Additional
zeros follow from centering or collinearity. The same calculation applies
to stem:[A_z] for correlation PCA.
====

Keeping only the first k singular values and their directions is called
*truncation*. It gives the previous PCA reconstruction. Its squared error
is the sum of omitted squared singular values, as checked in § 3.

*Optional numerical reference — outside today's required derivation.*
Why avoid forming the Gram matrix? *Conditioning* describes sensitivity to
small input changes. Let stem:[r_A=\operatorname{rank}(A_c)>0], the number
of nonzero singular values. Their largest-to-smallest ratio is the
*restricted condition number*, denoted stem:[\kappa_+]:

[stem]
++++
\kappa_+(A_c)=\frac{\sigma_1}{\sigma_{r_A}},\qquad
\kappa_+(A_c^\top A_c)=\kappa_+(A_c)^2.
++++

The Gram matrix has positive eigenvalues stem:[\sigma_j^2], which proves
the squared ratio. For example, a ratio 10⁴ in the data becomes 10⁸ in its
Gram matrix. Small directions become harder to compute accurately.
The ordinary condition number is infinite for a singular matrix; the finite
ratio above only concerns the nonzero directions. In floating-point arithmetic,
a threshold decides which singular values count as zero: this defines the
*numerical rank*. SVD works directly on the data, although it cannot recover
information below numerical precision.

In NumPy, `Vt` stores the transpose of V, `singular` its singular values,
`variances` the component variances, `ratios` their explained variance ratios
and `scores` the projected coordinates. The returned component directions
are sometimes called *loadings*; here the plotted direction coefficients
mean the entries of a unit vector, not a different scaled-loading convention.
`PC1` and `PC2` on English plots mean the first and second principal components.

eigenvalues = np.linalg.eigvalsh(S)

assert variances[k-1] - variances[k] > 1e-8  # separated retained subspace here
assert np.allclose(api.components_.T @ api.components_, Vt[:k].T @ Vt[:k])
assert np.allclose(api.inverse_transform(api.transform(Az)), reconstructed_z)
print(pd.DataFrame({"variance": variances, "ratio": ratios,

== Optional perspective: PCA and model order reduction in M2

A *snapshot* is one recorded state of a system, for example all temperatures
at the mesh nodes of a numerical simulation at one time. Instead of six
sensor variables, a state may have thousands of coordinates. *Proper
orthogonal decomposition* (*POD*) also seeks a few modes that reconstruct
such states with small error; SVD supplies a shared computational idea.

The conventions can differ: snapshots are often columns rather than rows,
centering depends on whether the mean state should be retained separately,
and a physical inner product can replace the ordinary Euclidean one. Automatic
unit-variance scaling is not the defining step of POD. The M2/S3 model order
reduction course will develop reduced dynamical models; deriving reduced PDE
operators is outside today's lesson. This paragraph is optional reading, not
another required exercise.

*Regression* estimates a designated variable, the *response*, from other
variables, called *predictors* or explanatory variables. Let m be the number
of training observations and d the number of model-input columns. The
*design matrix* stem:[\mathbf X\in\mathbb R^{m\times d}] collects those
inputs. Bold stem:[\mathbf X] differs from the random variable X on 20 October
and from the exploratory data matrix A. Let stem:[y\in\mathbb R^m] collect
the observed responses and stem:[\beta\in\mathbb R^d] the model coefficients.
An *intercept* is a constant term, represented by a column of ones.

*Small example:* with one predictor x and an intercept, d=2 and row i of the
design is stem:[(1,x_i)]. Its estimated response is
stem:[\beta_0+\beta_1x_i]: stem:[\beta_0] is the intercept (level at x=0),
and stem:[\beta_1] is the slope (change in estimated response per unit of x).

*Ordinary least squares* (OLS) chooses coefficients to minimize the sum of
squared differences between estimates and observations. Write stem:[J(\beta)]
for this objective and stem:[\widehat\beta] for a minimizing coefficient vector.
The fitted response vector is stem:[\widehat y=\mathbf X\widehat\beta];
the *residual* vector stem:[e=y-\widehat y] is observed minus fitted.
The gradient stem:[\nabla_\beta J] collects the coordinate derivatives:

[stem]
++++
J(\beta)=\lVert\mathbf X\beta-y\rVert_2^2,\qquad
\min_\beta J(\beta),\qquad
\nabla_\beta J=2\mathbf X^\top(\mathbf X\beta-y).
++++

Here the design and response are held at their observed values. The hats
label fitted coefficients, not population parameters. Orthogonality is an
algebraic property of the least-squares minimum; it does not establish
unbiased coefficients, a correct conditional-mean model or causal effects.

The design's *column space* is all linear combinations of its columns.
*Full column rank* means those columns are linearly independent. Unlike PCA,
OLS uses a designated response: preserving input variance and minimizing
response error are different objectives.

.Proposition: Least squares projects onto the design column space
[.thm#thm:matrix-ols]
****
Every least-squares minimizer satisfies stem:[\mathbf X^\top e=0], with stem:[e=y-\mathbf X\widehat\beta]. The fitted vector is unique; coefficients require full column rank for uniqueness.
****

[%collapsible]
.Proof — Least squares projects onto the design column space
====

*Step 1.* Differentiate the objective as above and set the gradient to zero. The normal equations are stem:[\mathbf X^\top\mathbf X\widehat\beta=\mathbf X^\top y].

*Step 2.* The residual stem:[e=y-\mathbf X\widehat\beta] obeys
stem:[\mathbf X^\top e=0]. Every column-space vector is stem:[\mathbf Xh]
for some coefficient vector stem:[h\in\mathbb R^d], and stem:[e^\top\mathbf Xh=0]. Therefore,
for any other coefficient vector stem:[\gamma\in\mathbb R^d], Pythagoras gives

[stem]
++++
\lVert y-\mathbf X\gamma\rVert_2^2
=\lVert e+\mathbf X(\widehat\beta-\gamma)\rVert_2^2
=\lVert e\rVert_2^2+\lVert\mathbf X(\widehat\beta-\gamma)\rVert_2^2
\geq\lVert e\rVert_2^2.
++++

This proves global minimality. Equality requires the same fitted vector.

*Step 3.* Orthogonal projection onto a fixed subspace is unique. Coefficients are unique only for full column rank. With rank deficiency,
adding a vector in the design kernel leaves fitted values unchanged; among
the possible solutions, the SVD-based solver selects coefficients of smallest
Euclidean length (a minimum-norm solution).
====

For the one-predictor model above, let stem:[\bar x,\bar y] be the means
of the m observed pairs and stem:[x_c,y_c] their centered vectors.
Reuse 20 October's sample standard deviations stem:[s_x,s_y], covariance
stem:[s_{xy}] and Pearson stem:[r_{xy}], now with divisor m−1.
Assume both sample variances are positive when using Pearson. The slope
stem:[\widehat\beta_1] and intercept stem:[\widehat\beta_0] have the following form.

.Proposition: The simple-regression slope is scaled Pearson
[.thm#thm:matrix-slope]
****
With a nonconstant predictor and an intercept, stem:[\widehat\beta_1=s_{xy}/s_x^2]
and stem:[\widehat\beta_0=\bar y-\widehat\beta_1\bar x]. If the response
is also nonconstant, stem:[\widehat\beta_1=r_{xy}s_y/s_x].
****

[%collapsible]
.Proof — The simple-regression slope is scaled Pearson
====

*Step 1.* For simple regression with intercept, differentiate with respect to the
intercept to obtain stem:[\widehat\beta_0=\bar y-\widehat\beta_1\bar x].

*Step 2.* Substitute this into the objective: it becomes
stem:[\sum_i(y_{c,i}-\beta_1 x_{c,i})^2].

*Step 3.* Differentiating again yields

[stem]
++++
\widehat\beta_1=\frac{x_c^\top y_c}{x_c^\top x_c}
=\frac{s_{xy}}{s_x^2}=r_{xy}\frac{s_y}{s_x},\qquad s_x>0,\ s_y>0.
++++

The covariance normalization cancels; a correlation coefficient is not a
slope because slope retains response/predictor units. This is 20 October's
population projection with finite sums replacing expectations. If the
predictor is constant, slope and intercept cannot be separately identified.
====

Normal equations characterize the projection but forming the Gram matrix
squares conditioning. QR factors a full-column-rank design into a matrix with orthonormal columns
and an upper triangular matrix (zeros below the diagonal). QR or SVD avoids that
step. Use `np.linalg.lstsq`, inspect rank and singular values, and avoid an
explicit inverse. No Gaussian assumption is needed to compute this fit;
uncertainty or causal interpretations need additional assumptions.

*Numerical example:* reuse the four observed pairs, x in hours and y in points.
The fitted line has intercept 0.5 point and slope 0.8 point/hour. The code
checks this slope and the residual orthogonality. `X_design` is the design;
`lstsq` returns coefficients, residual sums of squares (possibly an empty
array, depending on dimensions and rank), rank and singular values. The
residual vector itself is `y - X_design @ beta`.

*How will we compare errors?* A *baseline* is a simple reference rule: here,
always predict the training-response mean. It is the constant that minimizes
squared training error. *Coverage* reports how many available rows remain
complete (give both counts and their proportion). For b validation rows, let
stem:[y_i] be the observed value and stem:[\widehat y_i] its estimate.
*RMSE* means root mean squared error:

[stem]
++++
\operatorname{RMSE}=\sqrt{\frac1b\sum_{i=1}^b(y_i-\widehat y_i)^2}.
++++

RMSE has the response's units; a smaller value means a smaller mean squared
error on these same rows. It does not by itself establish accuracy on other dates.
A *preprocessing leak* would use validation information to learn a transformation,
for example centering by a mean computed on all fourteen days.
In the code, `mu_train` and `sd_train` are predictor means and sample standard
deviations learned on training rows; both partitions use those same values.
`Atr` and `Ava` are the standardized training and validation inputs;
`Xtr` and `Xva` add the intercept column. `ytr` and `yva` are their responses.
The variable `prediction` is the fitted model applied to validation inputs.

== 6. Optional preparation: high variance can be irrelevant to prediction

Let stem:[u=(-1,-1,1,1)^\top] and stem:[v=(-1,1,-1,1)^\top]. These centered
vectors are orthogonal and have the same variance, 4/3. Construct predictors
100u and v, and choose the response y=v, the small-variance second predictor. The leading PCA direction preserves the large
variance coordinate and loses the entire response signal. This is an exact
controlled counterexample, not a claim about Beijing.

v = np.array([-1., 1., -1., 1.])

_, ss, vv = np.linalg.svd(example, full_matrices=False)
first_score = example @ vv[0]
assert abs(first_score @ v) < 1e-10
assert ss[0]**2 / np.sum(ss**2) > .999
print("Leading explained ratio:", ss[0]**2 / np.sum(ss**2))
print("Response = second feature; the first PC is orthogonal to it.")
