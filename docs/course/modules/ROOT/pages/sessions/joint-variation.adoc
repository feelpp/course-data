== Probability recall: model, sampling and observations

*Probabilistic model before the data.* Let stem:[(\Omega,\mathcal F,\mathbb P)]
be the underlying probability space: stem:[\Omega] lists possible outcomes,
stem:[\mathcal F] specifies the events to which probabilities are assigned,
and stem:[\mathbb P] assigns those probabilities. We do not need further
measure theory here. A generic real random pair stem:[(X,Y)] has joint law
stem:[P_{XY}]; expectation stem:[\mathbb E] refers to this probability model.
Assume finite second moments, stem:[\mathbb E(X^2)<\infty] and
stem:[\mathbb E(Y^2)<\infty]. The population means and covariance are
*deterministic parameters*, generally unknown:

[stem]
++++
\mu_X=\mathbb E[X],\qquad \mu_Y=\mathbb E[Y],\qquad
\sigma_{XY}=\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)
=\mathbb E[(X-\mu_X)(Y-\mu_Y)].
++++

Variance is the special case Y=X:

[stem]
++++
\sigma_X^2=\operatorname{Var}(X)=\mathbb E[(X-\mu_X)^2].
++++

Fix an integer stem:[n\geq2]. Before observation, stem:[(X_i,Y_i)] is the
random pair with index stem:[i=1,\ldots,n]. Its actual observed values are
stem:[x_i=X_i(\omega_0)] and stem:[y_i=Y_i(\omega_0)], where
stem:[\omega_0\in\Omega] is the realized outcome of the whole sampling
experiment. The file contains these lowercase numerical values. We will
state the same-law and independence assumptions explicitly before the
unbiasedness proof; a numerical file does not establish them by itself.

*Check the objects:* a new sample can change the observed values and the
sample mean. It does not change the population parameter in the fixed model.

=== Basic expectation and covariance rules

For square-integrable random variables U,V,W and deterministic constants a,b,
linearity of expectation and bilinearity of covariance give

[stem]
++++
\mathbb E[aU+bV]=a\mathbb E[U]+b\mathbb E[V],\qquad
\operatorname{Cov}(aU+bV,W)
=a\operatorname{Cov}(U,W)+b\operatorname{Cov}(V,W).
++++

The second formula also holds in the second argument. Independence of U and V
gives stem:[\mathbb E(UV)=\mathbb E(U)\mathbb E(V)], hence zero covariance;
the converse does not hold. The proof below uses these rules on finite sums
of the random sample variables, not on already fixed numerical estimates.

.Proposition: Independence implies zero covariance
[.thm#thm:joint-independence]
****
For independent real random variables U and V with finite second moments,
stem:[\mathbb E(UV)=\mathbb E(U)\mathbb E(V)] and
stem:[\operatorname{Cov}(U,V)=0]. The converse is false: zero covariance
alone does not establish independence.
****

[%collapsible,id=joint-independence-proof]
.Proof — Independence, factorization and zero covariance
====
*Step 1 — Factor the joint probabilities.* To see the mechanism explicitly,
first suppose U and V take finitely many possible values, denoted by u and v.
Here stem:[\mathbb P(U=u,V=v)] is the probability that both equalities hold
in the same experiment. Independence means

[stem]
++++
\mathbb P(U=u,V=v)=\mathbb P(U=u)\mathbb P(V=v).
++++

*Step 2 — Compute the expectation of the product.* Each possible pair (u,v)
contributes its product uv, weighted by the probability of that pair. Insert
the factorization from Step 1, and then separate the two sums:

[stem]
++++
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb E[UV]
&=\sum_u\sum_v uv\,\mathbb P(U=u,V=v)\\
&=\sum_u\sum_v u\,\mathbb P(U=u)\;v\,\mathbb P(V=v)\\
&=\left(\sum_u u\,\mathbb P(U=u)\right)
  \left(\sum_v v\,\mathbb P(V=v)\right)\\
&=\mathbb E[U]\mathbb E[V].
\end{aligned}
++++

The factor stem:[u\mathbb P(U=u)] depends only on u, and
stem:[v\mathbb P(V=v)] only on v: the double sum is therefore the product
of the two sums. This calculation displays the mechanism for finite-valued
variables. The same expectation factorization holds for independent
variables with finite second moments in general; for variables with a
density, the analogous calculation uses integrals instead of sums.

*Step 3 — Expand the covariance.* Define the constants
stem:[\mu_U=\mathbb E(U)] and stem:[\mu_V=\mathbb E(V)]. First expand the
product, then use linearity of expectation, and finally replace each mean
by its definition:

[stem]
++++
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{Cov}(U,V)
&=\mathbb E[(U-\mu_U)(V-\mu_V)]\\
&=\mathbb E[UV-\mu_VU-\mu_UV+\mu_U\mu_V]\\
&=\mathbb E[UV]-\mu_V\mathbb E[U]-\mu_U\mathbb E[V]+\mu_U\mu_V\\
&=\mathbb E[UV]-\mu_V\mu_U-\mu_U\mu_V+\mu_U\mu_V\\
&=\mathbb E[UV]-\mu_U\mu_V.
\end{aligned}
++++

This identity holds without independence. Under independence, Step 2 gives
stem:[\mathbb E(UV)=\mu_U\mu_V], so

[stem]
++++
\operatorname{Cov}(U,V)=\mu_U\mu_V-\mu_U\mu_V=0.
++++

*Small example.* Let U and V record two independent fair coin tosses, with
1 for heads and 0 for tails. Then stem:[\mu_U=\mu_V=1/2]. The product UV
equals 1 only when both tosses are heads, with probability 1/4; otherwise
it equals 0. Thus stem:[\mathbb E(UV)=1/4=(1/2)(1/2)] and the covariance
is zero. Each centered product is nevertheless either +1/4 or −1/4:
independence makes their average over the probability law zero, not each
individual product. An observed finite sample need not have exactly zero
sample covariance.
====

=== I.i.d. means independent pairs with the same joint law

For the following estimation result, assume that
stem:[(X_1,Y_1),\ldots,(X_n,Y_n)] are *independent and identically distributed*
(*i.i.d.*) with joint law stem:[P_{XY}], finite second moments and fixed n≥2.

. *Same joint law:* each pair has the same means stem:[\mu_X,\mu_Y] and the
  same within-pair covariance stem:[\sigma_{XY}]. In particular,
  stem:[\operatorname{Cov}(X_i,Y_i)=\sigma_{XY}].
. *Independence between indices:* for stem:[i\ne j], the two random vectors
  stem:[(X_i,Y_i)] and stem:[(X_j,Y_j)] are independent. Consequently their
  components stem:[X_i] and stem:[Y_j] are independent, so
  stem:[\mathbb E(X_iY_j)=\mathbb E(X_i)\mathbb E(Y_j)]. Therefore

[stem]
++++
\operatorname{Cov}(X_i,Y_j)
=\mathbb E[X_iY_j]-\mathbb E[X_i]\mathbb E[Y_j]=0\qquad(i\ne j).
++++

Independence *between pairs* does not require independence *within a pair*:
temperature and dew point recorded together may be dependent. Let stem:[C_n]
be the n-by-n matrix whose (i,j) entry is stem:[\operatorname{Cov}(X_i,Y_j)].
The diagonal refers to the same observation index; the other entries refer
to different indices:

[stem]
++++
C_n=\begin{pmatrix}
\sigma_{XY}&0&\cdots&0\\
0&\sigma_{XY}&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&\cdots&\sigma_{XY}
\end{pmatrix}=\sigma_{XY}I_n.
++++

Here stem:[I_n] is the identity matrix of size n. Exactly n diagonal terms
remain. This matrix indexes *observations*, not the different measured
variables of the feature covariance matrix introduced in §§ 10–12.

*Pause:* Which assumption gives the same diagonal entries? Which assumption
sets the off-diagonal entries to zero? Could the latter hold automatically
for consecutive meteorological hours?

== 1. Observe pairs before summarizing

Each row records measurements at *one monitoring station during one hour*:
we call this a *station-hour*. To compare temperature and dew point, take both
measurements from that same row. These two values form a *paired observation*.
Pairing values from different hours would answer a different question.

In the previous investigation, a histogram or an ECDF described the values of
*one variable at a time*: which values occurred and how often. This is its
*distribution*. When we consider one variable separately within a dataset
containing several variables, we also call this its *marginal distribution*.
For example, the temperature histogram describes temperature alone; it does
not tell us which dew-point reading accompanied each temperature.

We now ask whether higher temperatures tend to occur at the same hours as
higher or lower dew points. This is what *joint movement* means here: how
the two measurements vary together across paired observations. Two separate
histograms cannot answer that question, because they do not show the pairing.
We compare paired recorded values, not changes between successive hours.
A *scatter plot* represents each pair by a point; the collection of points is
also called a *point cloud*.

Reuse the xref:./../foundations/data-quality-practical.adoc[previous audit report]
before interpreting that plot. Its checks establish the *key* (the columns
identifying one row, here station and timestamp), *validity* (whether recorded
values satisfy the declared rules), measurement units and *missingness*
(which measurements are absent). Use the report rather than repeating the audit.

A *complete pair* has both selected measurements available in the same row.
For each comparison, keep those pairs and leave out rows where either selected
measurement is missing. A missing value in an unrelated column does not exclude
the row from this particular comparison. *Imputation* means replacing a missing
value with an estimated one, for example a mean or an interpolated value.
We do not impute values in this descriptive exercise. Record how many pairs
remain: this is the sample size stem:[n], which determines the divisor used
in a calculation. This follows the audit's
{audit-denominators-link}.

== 2. Why center and multiply deviations?

Uncentered products mix the variables' mean levels with how their deviations
vary together. Centering
makes zero mean deviations; products are positive for deviations with the
same sign and negative for opposite signs. Averaging adds reinforcement and
cancellation. Symmetry follows from multiplication, and
stem:[\operatorname{Cov}(X,X)=\operatorname{Var}(X)]. The unit is the product
of the measurement units. Large covariance need not mean tight alignment.

*Define the statistic before computing it.* The random sample means are
stem:[\bar X=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^nX_i] and
stem:[\bar Y=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^nY_i]. Their realizations are numerical means:
stem:[\bar x=\bar X(\omega_0)=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^nx_i], and similarly for y.
The capital stem:[\bar X] is a random variable; stem:[\mu_X] is a fixed
population parameter. Even when stem:[\mathbb E(\bar X)=\mu_X], the two
objects are not interchangeable.

Define the random sum stem:[Q_{XY}], which depends on the fixed sample size n,
and its realized value stem:[q_{xy}]:

[stem]
++++
Q_{XY}=\sum_{i=1}^n(X_i-\bar X)(Y_i-\bar Y),\qquad
q_{xy}=Q_{XY}(\omega_0)=\sum_{i=1}^n(x_i-\bar x)(y_i-\bar y).
++++

Our notation for the observed calculations is stem:[c_n=q_{xy}/n] for the
covariance of the empirical distribution and stem:[s_{xy}=q_{xy}/(n-1)] for
the corrected sample covariance. Similarly,
stem:[s_x^2=\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)^2/(n-1)]. An *estimator* is a statistic of
the random sample; an *estimate* is its realized numerical value. Thus the
random estimator stem:[S_{XY}=Q_{XY}/(n-1)] realizes to
stem:[s_{xy}=S_{XY}(\omega_0)]. Defining this rule does not yet prove it unbiased.

== 3. Why n−1? Three different statistical objects

Three objects answer different questions:

[cols="2,3,3",options="header"]
|===
|Object |Definition |Question
|Population parameter
|stem:[\sigma_{XY}=\operatorname{Cov}_{P_{XY}}(X,Y)], an expectation under the population law.
|How do the model's two variables vary together?
|Covariance of the observed empirical law
|stem:[c_n=q_{xy}/n], a number computed from the observed pairs.
|What is the exact covariance of the probability distribution giving equal weight to these recorded pairs?
|Estimator of the population covariance
|stem:[S_{XY}=Q_{XY}/(n-1)], a random variable; its observed value is stem:[s_{xy}].
|What rule has the correct expectation under specified sampling assumptions?
|===

The *empirical probability measure* is
stem:[\widehat P_n=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^n\delta_{(x_i,y_i)}], where
stem:[\delta_{(x_i,y_i)}] puts all its probability at the recorded pair.
It assigns weight 1/n to each pair, counting repeated values with their
multiplicity. For the coordinate variables on these recorded pairs, its
covariance is exactly

[stem]
++++
\operatorname{Cov}_{\widehat P_n}(X,Y)
=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n(x_i-\bar x)(y_i-\bar y)=c_n.
++++

In this expression the distribution is the observed empirical law, not the
population law stem:[P_{XY}]. Division by n is correct for that question.

=== Sampling assumptions used below

Use the opening probability recap: fixed n≥2, common joint law, finite second
moments and independence between pairs. In particular, the cross-index matrix
has exactly n diagonal entries equal to the population covariance; its other
entries vanish by independence, not just by centering.

=== The unbiasedness proof: a random estimator, then its realized value

An estimator is *unbiased* when its expectation under repeated sampling equals
the population parameter. The statement concerns the random statistic
stem:[S_{XY}], not an expectation taken over the already fixed number stem:[s_{xy}].

.Proposition: Unbiased covariance under i.i.d. sampling
[.thm#thm:joint-unbiased]
****
Under the assumptions just stated, the estimator stem:[S_{XY}=Q_{XY}/(n-1)]
satisfies stem:[\mathbb E(S_{XY})=\sigma_{XY}]. Its observed value
stem:[s_{xy}=q_{xy}/(n-1)] is not guaranteed to equal stem:[\sigma_{XY}].
****

[%collapsible]
.Proof — Unbiased covariance under i.i.d. sampling
====
*Step 1 — Identify the random algebra.* Expand each centered deviation as
stem:[X_i-\bar X=(X_i-\mu_X)-(\bar X-\mu_X)], and likewise for Y.
The population means are fixed constants; the sample means are random.
Multiplication gives all four terms:

[stem]
++++
\begin{aligned}
Q_{XY}
={}&\sum_{i=1}^n(X_i-\mu_X)(Y_i-\mu_Y)\\
&-(\bar Y-\mu_Y)\sum_{i=1}^n(X_i-\mu_X)\\
&-(\bar X-\mu_X)\sum_{i=1}^n(Y_i-\mu_Y)\\
&+n(\bar X-\mu_X)(\bar Y-\mu_Y).
\end{aligned}
++++

*Step 2 — Collect the cross terms, without independence.* By the definition of
the sample mean, stem:[\sum_i(X_i-\mu_X)=n(\bar X-\mu_X)], and similarly for Y.
Each of the two subtracted terms is therefore
stem:[n(\bar X-\mu_X)(\bar Y-\mu_Y)]. The last term adds one copy back:

[stem]
++++
Q_{XY}=\sum_{i=1}^n(X_i-\mu_X)(Y_i-\mu_Y)
-n(\bar X-\mu_X)(\bar Y-\mu_Y).
++++

This is an algebraic identity. Neither independence nor taking expectations
was needed. It also holds for realized numbers with lowercase means.

*Step 3 — Locate exactly where the sampling assumptions enter.* Same joint
law gives stem:[\mathbb E(\bar X)=\mu_X], stem:[\mathbb E(\bar Y)=\mu_Y], and
an expectation stem:[n\sigma_{XY}] for the first sum. Hence

[stem]
++++
\mathbb E[Q_{XY}]=n\sigma_{XY}-n\operatorname{Cov}(\bar X,\bar Y).
++++

Bilinearity of covariance (expanding sums in either argument) gives

[stem]
++++
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{Cov}(\bar X,\bar Y)
&=\operatorname{Cov}\left(\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^nX_i,\frac1n\sum_{j=1}^nY_j\right)\\
&=\frac1{n^2}\sum_{i=1}^n\sum_{j=1}^n\operatorname{Cov}(X_i,Y_j)\\
&=\frac1{n^2}\left(\sum_{i=1}^n\sigma_{XY}+\sum_{i\ne j}0\right)
=\frac{n\sigma_{XY}}{n^2}=\frac{\sigma_{XY}}n.
\end{aligned}
++++

The off-diagonal zeros follow from independence between pairs, as derived
above; the n equal diagonal entries follow from their common joint law.

*Step 4 — Take the expectation of the estimator, not of a fixed estimate.*
Substitute this covariance of means:

[stem]
++++
\mathbb E[Q_{XY}]=n\sigma_{XY}-n\frac{\sigma_{XY}}n
=(n-1)\sigma_{XY},\qquad
\boxed{\mathbb E[S_{XY}]=\sigma_{XY}}.
++++

Across independent repetitions of the whole n-pair experiment, the estimator's
average approaches this expectation. One realized estimate can be above or
below the target. The theorem does not assert its accuracy on one file.
====

*Variance is the same calculation with Y=X.* In that special case
stem:[\sigma_{XX}=\sigma_X^2=\operatorname{Var}(X)] and
stem:[Q_{XX}=\sum_i(X_i-\bar X)^2]. The same expansion and
stem:[\operatorname{Var}(\bar X)=\sigma_X^2/n] yield

[stem]
++++
\mathbb E[Q_{XX}]=(n-1)\sigma_X^2,\qquad
S_{XX}=\frac{Q_{XX}}{n-1},\qquad
\mathbb E[S_{XX}]=\sigma_X^2.
++++

Its realized value is stem:[s_x^2=\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)^2/(n-1)].

*Why the other divisor remains legitimate.* Before realization, define
stem:[\widehat\sigma_{XY,n}=Q_{XY}/n]. Under the same i.i.d. assumptions,

[stem]
++++
\mathbb E[\widehat\sigma_{XY,n}]=\frac{n-1}{n}\sigma_{XY},\qquad
\operatorname{Bias}(\widehat\sigma_{XY,n})
=\mathbb E[\widehat\sigma_{XY,n}]-\sigma_{XY}=-\frac{\sigma_{XY}}n.
++++

Bias is expected estimator minus target. The divisor-n estimator's expectation
is contracted towards zero: its bias is negative for a positive covariance,
positive for a negative covariance, and zero when the covariance is zero.
The realized value stem:[c_n] is still the exact covariance of the empirical
law. These are two different questions about the same formula. Divisor n also
arises for covariance maximum likelihood in an unconstrained i.i.d. Gaussian
model with unknown mean; that is an optional separate estimation criterion.

*Connection to Beijing.* Consecutive station-hours can share weather patterns;
for different indices, stem:[\operatorname{Cov}(X_i,Y_j)] need not vanish.
Even if all indices had the same means and joint law, the correct intermediate
formula would retain every cross-time term:

[stem]
++++
\mathbb E[Q_{XY}]=n\sigma_{XY}
-\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\sum_{j=1}^n\operatorname{Cov}(X_i,Y_j).
++++

With differing station or period distributions even the common-law assumption
needs justification. The descriptive calculation stem:[s_{xy}] remains
computable, but the i.i.d. unbiasedness conclusion does not transfer unchanged.
Selecting complete pairs also does not establish representative sampling from
an original population: missingness and the sampling protocol matter.

rng = np.random.default_rng(20261020)
repetitions = 20000
true_covariance, true_variance = 0.7, 1.0
sizes = [5, 20, 100]
mc_rows = []
fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 3, figsize=(11, 3.5), constrained_layout=True)
for ax, sample_size in zip(axes, sizes, strict=True):
    draws = rng.multivariate_normal([0., 0.], [[1., .7], [.7, 1.]],
                                   size=(repetitions, sample_size))
    centered = draws - draws.mean(axis=1, keepdims=True)
    q_rep = (centered[:, :, 0] * centered[:, :, 1]).sum(axis=1)
    squared_rep = (centered[:, :, 0]**2).sum(axis=1)
    for ddof in [0, 1]:
        covariance = q_rep / (sample_size - ddof)
        variance = squared_rep / (sample_size - ddof)
        factor = (sample_size - 1) / (sample_size - ddof)
        # NumPy conventions, checked on one realized dataset.
        assert np.isclose(covariance[0], np.cov(draws[0], rowvar=False, ddof=ddof)[0, 1])
        assert np.isclose(variance[0], draws[0, :, 0].var(ddof=ddof))
        mc_rows.append({"n": sample_size, "ddof": ddof,
                        "MC covariance mean": covariance.mean(),
                        "theoretical covariance mean": factor * true_covariance,
                        "MC variance mean": variance.mean(),
                        "theoretical variance mean": factor * true_variance})
        ax.hist(covariance, bins=50, density=True, histtype="step",
                linestyle="--" if ddof == 0 else "-", label=f"ddof={ddof}")
    ax.axvline(true_covariance, color="black", label="Population covariance")
    ax.set(xlabel="Estimated covariance", ylabel="Density", title=f"n={sample_size}")
axes[-1].legend(fontsize=7)
print(pd.DataFrame(mc_rows).round(5).to_string(index=False))
print("Monte Carlo averages approximate expectations; the algebra is the proof.")
plt.show()

== 4. Units motivate Pearson; transformations have different purposes

Define the population standard deviations stem:[\sigma_X=\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(X)}] and stem:[\sigma_Y=\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(Y)}], and assume both are positive. Define

[stem]
++++
\rho_{XY}=\frac{\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)}{\sigma_X\sigma_Y},\qquad
Z_X=\frac{X-\mu_X}{\sigma_X},\quad Z_Y=\frac{Y-\mu_Y}{\sigma_Y}.
++++

.Proposition: Pearson is covariance after standardization
[.thm#thm:joint-standardization]
****
For the population-standardized variables just defined, stem:[\operatorname{Cov}(Z_X,Z_Y)=\rho_{XY}].
****

[%collapsible]
.Proof — Pearson is covariance after standardization
====

*Step 1.* Linearity of expectation gives stem:[\mathbb E(Z_X)=(\mathbb E(X)-\mu_X)/\sigma_X=0], and similarly for Y.

*Step 2.* Their covariance is therefore stem:[\mathbb E(Z_XZ_Y)]. Pulling the constant denominator outside the expectation gives
stem:[\operatorname{Cov}(Z_X,Z_Y)=\rho_{XY}]. Standardization removes the
product of units and makes each variance one. It does not linearize a curve,
remove outliers, or make a distribution Gaussian.
====

[cols="2,2,3",options="header"]
|===
|Operation |Observed vector |Purpose and condition
|Centering |stem:[x_c=x-\bar x\mathbf1] |Remove the level; preserve units.
|Scaling |stem:[a x] |Change units or relative weights.
|Standardization |stem:[x_c/s_x] |Sample variance one, divisor n−1; requires stem:[s_x>0].
|Min-max |stem:[(x-\min x)/(\max x-\min x)] |Observed range [0,1]; requires nonzero range.
|Euclidean normalization |stem:[x/\lVert x\rVert_2] |Length one; requires nonzero vector, does not center.
|===

For a nonconstant observed pair, sample Pearson is
stem:[r_{xy}=s_{xy}/(s_xs_y)]. Using the same divisor in all three quantities
also gives the same ratio with stem:[n]. The observed Pearson coefficient estimates population correlation only under
an appropriate sampling model. The unbiasedness result for stem:[S_{XY}] does
not imply unbiasedness of Pearson: taking a ratio is a nonlinear operation.
Sample standard deviations with
stem:[\texttt{ddof}=1] and population-law standard deviations are different
objects. NumPy's default `std()` uses `ddof=0`; state your convention.

An *affine transformation* has the form stem:[aX+b], with fixed real numbers
a and b: multiply by a scale factor, then add an offset. Changing °C to °F is
one example. We now check what such changes do to correlation.

.Proposition: Affine rescaling and constants
[.thm#thm:joint-affine]
****
For fixed real stem:[a,b,c,d] with stem:[ac\ne0], stem:[\operatorname{Corr}(aX+b,cY+d)=\operatorname{sign}(ac)\rho_{XY}]. Constant variables have undefined Pearson correlation.
****

[%collapsible]
.Proof — Affine rescaling and constants
====
*Proof of affine behavior:* For nonzero stem:[a,c], centering
stem:[aX+b] gives stem:[a(X-\mu_X)], so covariance multiplies by stem:[ac]
and standard deviations by stem:[|a|,|c|]. Thus
stem:[\operatorname{Corr}(aX+b,cY+d)=\operatorname{sign}(ac)\rho_{XY}].
Positive rescalings preserve the sign, one negative rescaling reverses it.
Constants have zero denominator: their Pearson correlation is undefined.
====

== 5. Pearson is a cosine in observation space

Define stem:[x_c=x-\bar x\mathbf1] and stem:[y_c=y-\bar y\mathbf1], where stem:[\mathbf1] is the n-vector of ones. Let stem:[\lVert u\rVert_2=\sqrt{u^\top u}] and let stem:[\theta] be the angle between these nonzero centered vectors. Cancel stem:[n-1]:

[stem]
++++
r_{xy}=\frac{x_c^\top y_c}{\lVert x_c\rVert_2\lVert y_c\rVert_2}
=\cos\theta.
++++

.Proposition: Pearson bounds and equality cases
[.thm#thm:joint-cosine]
****
For two nonconstant complete observed vectors, stem:[-1\leq r_{xy}\leq1]. Equality means proportional centered vectors; zero means orthogonality.
****

[%collapsible]
.Proof — Pearson bounds and equality cases
====

*Step 1.* Cauchy–Schwarz gives stem:[|x_c^\top y_c|\leq\lVert x_c\rVert\lVert y_c\rVert],
hence stem:[-1\leq r_{xy}\leq1] after division by the positive product of norms.

*Step 2.* Equality in Cauchy–Schwarz holds exactly when the two
nonzero centered vectors are proportional: stem:[y_c=a x_c], with positive
stem:[a] for +1 and negative stem:[a] for −1. This is an exact affine
relationship in the observed pairs. Zero correlation is orthogonality
(a right angle, with zero dot product), not
absence of every possible relationship.
====

def centered_pearson(left, right):
    """Finite complete vectors; a constant has undefined Pearson correlation."""
    left, right = np.asarray(left, dtype=float), np.asarray(right, dtype=float)
    if left.ndim != 1 or left.shape != right.shape or len(left) < 2:
        raise ValueError("Use equally sized vectors with at least two paired values")
    if not (np.isfinite(left).all() and np.isfinite(right).all()):
        raise ValueError("Select complete pairs first")
    lc, rc = left - left.mean(), right - right.mean()
    denominator = np.linalg.norm(lc) * np.linalg.norm(rc)
    return np.nan if denominator == 0 else (lc @ rc) / denominator

print("Sample Pearson:", centered_pearson(x, y))

=== Optional reading: the centering constraint and the n−1 dimension

This geometric view complements the expectation proof; it does not replace
its sampling assumptions. Let stem:[I_n] be the identity and stem:[\mathbf1]
the column of n ones. Define the *centering matrix*

[stem]
++++
H=I_n-\frac1n\mathbf1\mathbf1^\top.
++++

It acts on the observed vectors as stem:[x_c=Hx] and stem:[y_c=Hy]. Let
stem:[\mathbf1^\perp=\{v\in\mathbb R^n:\mathbf1^\top v=0\}] be the space
of vectors whose coordinates sum to zero. The *rank* of H is its image's
dimension; its *trace* stem:[\operatorname{tr}(H)] is the sum of its diagonal entries.

.Proposition: Centering projects onto an n−1 dimensional space
[.thm#thm:joint-centering]
****
H is symmetric and idempotent, stem:[H^\top=H] and stem:[H^2=H]. Its image is
stem:[\mathbf1^\perp], and stem:[\operatorname{rank}(H)=\operatorname{tr}(H)=n-1].
****

[%collapsible]
.Proof — Centering, one constraint, rank and trace
====
*Step 1.* Let stem:[P=\mathbf1\mathbf1^\top/n]. Because
stem:[\mathbf1^\top\mathbf1=n], direct multiplication gives stem:[P^2=P]
and transposition gives stem:[P^\top=P]. Thus
stem:[H^\top=H] and stem:[H^2=(I_n-P)^2=I_n-2P+P^2=H].

*Step 2.* We have stem:[H\mathbf1=0]. For every vector v with
stem:[\mathbf1^\top v=0], stem:[Hv=v]. So H removes the constant component
and keeps exactly the zero-sum vectors: it is their orthogonal projector.

*Step 3.* The constraint stem:[\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)=0] fixes the last centered
coordinate once the first n−1 are given. Thus this subspace has dimension
n−1 and H has rank n−1. Its n diagonal entries are each 1−1/n, so
stem:[\operatorname{tr}(H)=n(1-1/n)=n-1]. Estimating the mean has removed
one constant direction; this is the geometric degree of freedom.

*Step 4 — Optional connection to the expectation.* Let
stem:[\mathbf X=(X_1,\ldots,X_n)^\top] and
stem:[\mathbf Y=(Y_1,\ldots,Y_n)^\top] be the random sample columns.
Then stem:[Q_{XY}=\mathbf X^\top H\mathbf Y]. Under the i.i.d. assumptions
already proved, stem:[\mathbb E(X_iY_j)] equals stem:[\mu_X\mu_Y] off the
diagonal and stem:[\sigma_{XY}+\mu_X\mu_Y] on it. Therefore

[stem]
++++
\mathbb E[Q_{XY}]=\sigma_{XY}\sum_iH_{ii}
+\mu_X\mu_Y\sum_{i,j}H_{ij}
=\sigma_{XY}\operatorname{tr}(H)=(n-1)\sigma_{XY},
++++

because stem:[\sum_{i,j}H_{ij}=\mathbf1^\top H\mathbf1=0]. The trace explains
where the factor appears geometrically; independence and common law still
supply the required cross-moments.
====

*Bridge to the matrix sections below:* for an observed table A with n rows, centering every
column gives stem:[A_c=HA]. Since H is symmetric and idempotent, its observed
sample covariance matrix can also be written
stem:[S=A_c^\top A_c/(n-1)=A^\top HA/(n-1)]. No randomness is needed for
this matrix identity.

== 6. Why does covariance select a linear relationship?

.Proposition: Best affine approximation is an orthogonal projection
[.thm#thm:joint-projection]
****
For stem:[\operatorname{Var}(X)>0], the centered approximation stem:[a^\star U] minimizing stem:[\mathbb E((V-aU)^2)] has stem:[a^\star=\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)/\operatorname{Var}(X)] and residual orthogonal to U. Here stem:[U=X-\mathbb E(X)] and stem:[V=Y-\mathbb E(Y)].
****

[%collapsible]
.Proof — Best affine approximation is an orthogonal projection
====
Centering an affine response yields stem:[aX+b-\mathbb E(aX+b)=a(X-\mu_X)],
so stem:[\operatorname{Cov}(X,aX+b)=a\operatorname{Var}(X)]. More generally,
put stem:[U=X-\mathbb E(X)] and stem:[V=Y-\mathbb E(Y)]. The space of
centered random variables with finite expected squares is a vector space.
The name *square-integrable*, or stem:[L^2], means that
stem:[\mathbb E(W^2)<\infty] for a random variable W. We identify two variables
when they are equal *almost surely*, meaning with probability one. The inner product is
stem:[\langle U,V\rangle=\mathbb E(UV)] and squared norm is variance.
This identification makes the inner product positive definite:
stem:[\mathbb E(W^2)=0] means that W is zero with probability one.

Which multiple of stem:[U] best approximates stem:[V]? For stem:[\operatorname{Var}(X)>0],

[stem]
++++
\begin{aligned}
J(a)&=\mathbb E[(V-aU)^2]
=\mathbb E[V^2]-2a\mathbb E[UV]+a^2\mathbb E[U^2],\\
J'(a)&=-2\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)+2a\operatorname{Var}(X),\\
a^\star&=\frac{\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)}{\operatorname{Var}(X)}.
\end{aligned}
++++

*Step 1.* Expansion of the squared residual gives the quadratic above.

*Step 2.* Differentiation gives the stationary coefficient. Its second derivative is stem:[J^{\prime\prime}(a)=2\operatorname{Var}(X)>0], so this is the unique minimum.

*Step 3.* Substitute this coefficient into stem:[\mathbb E(U(V-a^\star U))] to obtain zero. The residual
stem:[e=V-a^\star U] satisfies stem:[\mathbb E(Ue)=0]; this is the orthogonal
projection of stem:[V] onto the line spanned by stem:[U]. Restoring means
gives the best affine predictor stem:[\mu_Y+a^\star(X-\mu_X)]. No Gaussian
assumption is needed for this projection identity. A linear approximation
need not equal the *conditional mean* stem:[\mathbb E(Y\mid X)], the expected
value of Y when X is known: that dependence may follow a curve rather than a line.

*Bridge:* Replacing the expectation inner product by the finite dot product
gives simple least squares. the 2 November lesson generalizes the line to a column space.
====

== 8. Limits: a curve can be perfectly dependent and uncorrelated

.Proposition: Zero Pearson does not imply independence
[.thm#thm:joint-nonlinear]
****
There are nonconstant variables with deterministic dependence and zero Pearson correlation. Take stem:[X\sim\mathcal U(-1,1)] and stem:[Y=X^2].
****

[%collapsible]
.Proof — Zero Pearson does not imply independence
====

*Step 1.* Let stem:[X\sim\mathcal U(-1,1)] and stem:[Y=X^2]. For an integrable function g, stem:[\mathbb E(g(X))=\tfrac12\int_{-1}^1g(x)\,dx]. Symmetry of the odd integrands gives
stem:[\mathbb E(X)=\mathbb E(X^3)=0], hence
stem:[\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)=\mathbb E(X^3)-\mathbb E(X)\mathbb E(X^2)=0].

*Step 2.* Compute the even moments stem:[\mathbb E(X^2)=1/3] and stem:[\mathbb E(X^4)=1/5]. Both variances are positive: stem:[\operatorname{Var}(X)=1/3] and
stem:[\operatorname{Var}(Y)=1/5-1/9=4/45]. Thus Pearson is zero although
stem:[Y] is a nonconstant deterministic function of stem:[X]. For example,
the identical events stem:[\{|X|\leq1/2\}] and stem:[\{Y\leq1/4\}] each have
probability 1/2; their intersection has probability 1/2 rather than 1/4.

*Step 3.* Independence would require the intersection probability to equal the product. It does not; this directly disproves independence. Independence implies zero covariance
under the stated moments; the converse fails.
====

== 10. One table, two geometric spaces

Let n be the number of observations, with stem:[n>1], and p the number of
selected numeric variables (also called *features*). The data matrix is
stem:[A\in\mathbb R^{n\times p}]: n rows and p columns. Its entry stem:[A_{ij}]
is the *observed numerical value* of variable j in observation i, where stem:[i=1,\ldots,n] and
stem:[j=1,\ldots,p]. A *complete observation* has a recorded value for every
selected variable. This extends the complete-pair rule above to p variables;
we remove incomplete rows rather than fill missing values with estimates
(*imputation*). Use the xref:./../foundations/data-quality-practical.adoc[audit]
to check keys, valid values, units and missing entries before this selection.

A is a realized table, held fixed in the algebra below. Its uppercase letter
follows the matrix convention and does not by itself declare a random variable.
Likewise S and R below are observed sample matrices; they are not population
parameters. We introduce their random counterparts separately.

A row is a point in *feature space* stem:[\mathbb R^p], the space of p numeric
coordinates. A column is a vector in *observation space* stem:[\mathbb R^n],
one coordinate per row. For example:

[stem]
++++
A=\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\3&4\\5&6\end{pmatrix},\qquad n=3,\quad p=2.
++++

The first row is the point (1,2); the first column is the vector (1,3,5).
Changing a unit stretches one feature axis. Centering subtracts each column's
mean, moving the row cloud so its mean point is zero.

*Notation before the calculation.* Superscript stem:[\top] transposes a matrix
or vector. For vectors u and v of the same length, stem:[u^\top v=\sum_i u_iv_i]
is their Euclidean inner product; stem:[\lVert u\rVert_2=\sqrt{u^\top u}]
is length. Orthogonal means this product is zero. Let stem:[I_n] be the
identity matrix of size n, stem:[\mathbf1\in\mathbb R^n] the column of ones,
and stem:[\bar a\in\mathbb R^p] the vector of column means, with
stem:[\bar a_j=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^n A_{ij}]. Define H as the centering matrix
and stem:[A_c] as the centered table:

[stem]
++++
H=I_n-\frac1n\mathbf1\mathbf1^\top,\qquad
A_c=HA=A-\mathbf1\bar a^\top.
++++

In the small example, stem:[\bar a=(3,4)^\top] and the centered rows are
(−2,−2), (0,0), (2,2). An *orthogonal projection* keeps the component in a
subspace and removes a perpendicular component. Here we remove the constant
part of each column. The notation stem:[\mathbf1^\perp] means all vectors
orthogonal to stem:[\mathbf1], namely those whose coordinates sum to zero.
*Symmetric* means unchanged by transposition; *idempotent* means that applying
the same matrix twice has the same effect as applying it once.

.Proposition: Centering is an orthogonal projection
[.thm#thm:matrix-centering]
****
The matrix H is symmetric and idempotent: stem:[H^\top=H], stem:[H^2=H]. It projects orthogonally onto stem:[\mathbf1^\perp], the vectors whose coordinates sum to zero.
****

[%collapsible]
.Proof — Centering is an orthogonal projection
====
Let stem:[P=\mathbf1\mathbf1^\top/n] keep the constant component of a column.
Its entries are all 1/n, so applying P gives the column mean in every position.

*Step 1.* Since stem:[\mathbf1^\top\mathbf1=n],
stem:[P^2=P] and stem:[P^\top=P].

*Step 2.* Subtract P from the identity. Consequently
stem:[H^\top=H] and stem:[H^2=I-2P+P^2=H].

*Step 3.* The image is the set of possible outputs. For any column vector
stem:[u\in\mathbb R^n],
stem:[H\mathbf1=0] and stem:[Hu=u] whenever stem:[\mathbf1^\top u=0].
Thus H projects orthogonally onto stem:[\mathbf1^\perp]. This space has
dimension n−1: its last coordinate is determined by the first n−1. In code,
NumPy subtracts a row of means from every row (broadcasting); storing H
instead would require n² entries.
====

In the Beijing table, PM2.5
and PM10 are particulate concentrations (µg/m³); TEMP is temperature and DEWP
the dew-point temperature at which condensation starts (°C); PRES is pressure
(hPa), and WSPM wind speed (m/s). A station-hour means one station observed

== 11. Covariance is a Gram matrix

A *Gram matrix* collects all pairwise inner products between a set of vectors.
Here those vectors are the centered columns. On this *one common set of complete
rows*, define S, the sample covariance matrix; j and k index variables and i
indexes observations:

[stem]
++++
S=\frac1{n-1}A_c^\top A_c=\frac1{n-1}A^\top HA\in\mathbb R^{p\times p},\qquad
S_{jk}=\frac1{n-1}\sum_i(A_{ij}-\bar a_j)(A_{ik}-\bar a_k).
++++

The second equality uses stem:[H^\top H=H]. It is a finite-table identity,
independent of any sampling assumption.

*When is this an estimator of a population matrix?* Let
stem:[\mathbf W=(W_1,\ldots,W_p)^\top] be a generic random observation with
finite second moments; here j indexes variables. Its population mean is
stem:[\mu=\mathbb E(\mathbf W)], and its deterministic covariance matrix
stem:[\Gamma] has entries stem:[\Gamma_{jk}=\operatorname{Cov}(W_j,W_k)].
The letter stem:[\Gamma] distinguishes population covariance from the singular-value matrix used on 2 November.
Let stem:[\mathbf W_i] be the random vector for sample index i, so that
stem:[A_{ij}=W_{ij}(\omega_0)] is its observed j-th entry. Define the random
sample mean and covariance estimator:

[stem]
++++
\bar{\mathbf W}=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\mathbf W_i,\qquad
\widehat\Gamma_n=\frac1{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^n
(\mathbf W_i-\bar{\mathbf W})(\mathbf W_i-\bar{\mathbf W})^\top.
++++

For i.i.d. random vectors, the scalar proof above applied to every pair of
components gives stem:[\mathbb E(\widehat\Gamma_n)=\Gamma]. The computed
matrix is its realized estimate stem:[S=\widehat\Gamma_n(\omega_0)], not a
matrix whose entries are guaranteed to equal the population values. The
complete-row selection and the sampling assumptions require separate justification.
The Gram, centering and PCA identities for the observed table remain valid
without that unbiasedness claim.

The diagonal entry stem:[S_{jj}=s_j^2] is the corrected sample variance of
column j, with stem:[s_j] its sample standard deviation. A symmetric matrix
is *positive semidefinite* when stem:[v^\top Sv\geq0] for every vector v.
It is *positive definite* if the inequality is strict for every nonzero v.
The *rank* is the number of linearly independent columns. The *kernel*
stem:[\ker(B)] of a matrix B is the set of vectors v such that stem:[Bv=0].
These definitions explain the next result, rather than assuming invertibility.

*Small example:* our centered table has two identical columns, so its rank is
one and stem:[A_c(1,-1)^\top=0]. Here
stem:[S=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}4&4\\4&4\end{smallmatrix}\right)].
It is positive semidefinite but singular: a square matrix is *singular* when
its rank is smaller than its size, so it has no inverse. Collinear columns
(proportional vectors) are one possible reason.

.Proposition: A centered Gram matrix is positive semidefinite
[.thm#thm:matrix-gram]
****
For stem:[n>1], S is symmetric positive semidefinite with stem:[\operatorname{rank}(S)\leq\min(p,n-1)] and diagonal stem:[S_{jj}=s_j^2].
****

[%collapsible]
.Proof — A centered Gram matrix is positive semidefinite
====
The diagonal stem:[S_{jj}=s_j^2] contains sample variances.

*Step 1.* Transposing the product proves symmetry.

*Step 2.* For every stem:[v\in\mathbb R^p],

[stem]
++++
v^\top Sv=\frac{\lVert A_cv\rVert_2^2}{n-1}\geq0.
++++

Thus S is positive semidefinite, not necessarily positive definite.

*Step 3.* If stem:[Sv=0], the norm identity forces stem:[A_cv=0]. Conversely, stem:[A_cv=0] implies stem:[Sv=0] by the product formula. The kernels are equal. The rank-nullity identity says that the number of
columns equals rank plus kernel dimension; both matrices have p columns,
so they have equal ranks. Since stem:[A_c=HA] and H has rank n−1,
stem:[\operatorname{rank}(S)=\operatorname{rank}(A_c)\leq\min(p,n-1)].
Centering loses a degree of freedom, and collinear features may lose more.
These are algebraic identities for a finite table, independent of any i.i.d.
assumption. Calling S an unbiased population estimator needs the sampling assumptions stated above.
====

*Pairwise deletion* drops missing values separately for each pair of variables.
It can use a different table for each covariance entry and
destroy this Gram structure. A symmetric pairwise-complete covariance matrix
need not be positive semidefinite. Do not mix that construction with this proof.

== 12. Correlation changes the geometry

Why reduce the columns? A distance between rows adds squared coordinate
differences. With mixed units, the variable with the largest numerical scale
can dominate it. A *metric* is a rule for measuring distances; choosing scales
changes this rule.

The notation stem:[\operatorname{diag}] builds a matrix with the supplied
values on its diagonal and zeros elsewhere. If all variances are positive, define
stem:[D=\operatorname{diag}(s_1^2,\ldots,s_p^2)] and
stem:[A_z=A_cD^{-1/2}], the sample-standardized table. Here
stem:[D^{-1/2}=\operatorname{diag}(1/s_1,\ldots,1/s_p)]: multiply each
centered column j by stem:[1/s_j]. Let R denote the correlation matrix. Then

[stem]
++++
R=D^{-1/2}SD^{-1/2}=\frac1{n-1}A_z^\top A_z.
++++

.Proposition: The correlation matrix is a standardized Gram matrix
[.thm#thm:matrix-correlation]
****
With positive variances, stem:[R=A_z^\top A_z/(n-1)] and stem:[R_{jj}=1]. Each off-diagonal entry is sample Pearson on the same complete rows.
****

[%collapsible]
.Proof — The correlation matrix is a standardized Gram matrix
====

*Step 1.* Substitute stem:[A_z=A_cD^{-1/2}] into its Gram product. Since the diagonal scaling is symmetric, stem:[A_z^\top A_z/(n-1)=D^{-1/2}SD^{-1/2}].

*Step 2.* Its j-th diagonal entry is stem:[S_{jj}/s_j^2=1]; off-diagonal entries are stem:[S_{jk}/(s_js_k)]. R is the covariance matrix of *sample-standardized*
columns, and each off-diagonal entry is a Pearson coefficient. The same
complete observations must be used in S, D and R. Constant columns cannot be
standardized and require a declared removal or separate treatment.

Standardizing gives every variable unit sample variance and changes distances
between observations. A small-variance but noisy measurement can then receive
substantial weight. The 2 November lesson examines how this scaling choice
affects a low-dimensional summary of the table.

====
