Means and variances describe centre and dispersion, but many decisions ask a different question: how large is a high but plausible outcome, or how often will a consequential limit be exceeded? This lesson develops quantiles and exceedance probabilities from the cumulative distribution function, then separates population variation from estimation uncertainty.

== Learning outcomes

After this lesson, you should be able to:

. calculate and interpret a CDF, survival probability, and generalized quantile;
. distinguish stem:[X>u] from stem:[X\geq u] for distributions with point masses;
. estimate an exceedance probability and its sampling uncertainty;
. explain why extreme quantiles require substantially more data than central summaries;
. distinguish an outcome threshold from a classifier decision threshold and a prediction interval from a confidence interval.

== Cumulative and survival probabilities

Let stem:[X] have cumulative distribution function stem:[F].

.Definition: CDF and survival function
[.def#def:cdf-survival]
****
[stem]
++++
F(x)=\mathbb P(X\leq x),
\qquad
S(x)=\mathbb P(X>x)=1-F(x).
++++

For a threshold stem:[u], stem:[S(u)] is the *strict exceedance probability*. If the event is stem:[X\geq u], then

[stem]
++++
\mathbb P(X\geq u)=1-F(u^-),
\qquad
F(u^-)=\lim_{x\uparrow u}F(x).
++++
****

The distinction between stem:[>] and stem:[\geq] disappears for a continuous distribution but matters for counts, rounded measurements, detection limits, and mixed distributions. Every operational requirement must state the convention.

For a density stem:[f], the CDF and survival probability are integrals:

[stem]
++++
F(u)=\int_{-\infty}^{u}f(x)\,dx,
\qquad
S(u)=\int_u^{\infty}f(x)\,dx.
++++

Numerical libraries often implement `sf` directly. In far tails it can be more accurate than subtracting a CDF value extremely close to one from one.

== Quantiles are inverse probability statements

.Definition: Generalized quantile
[.def#def:generalized-quantile]
****
For stem:[0<p<1], the lower stem:[p]-quantile is

[stem]
++++
Q(p)=\inf\{x\in\mathbb R:F(x)\geq p\}.
++++
****

This generalized inverse works for continuous and discrete distributions. For a continuous strictly increasing CDF, stem:[F(Q(p))=p]. With jumps or flat regions, equality and uniqueness can fail; the definition still selects a valid lower quantile. The median is stem:[Q(0.5)], and the interquartile range is stem:[Q(0.75)-Q(0.25)].

Quantiles preserve order under a continuous strictly increasing transformation stem:[g]:

[stem]
++++
Q_{g(X)}(p)=g\!\left(Q_X(p)\right).
++++

This is one reason quantiles are useful for skewed measurements and nonlinear unit changes. In contrast, stem:[\mathbb E(g(X))] is generally not stem:[g(\mathbb E(X))].

*Interpreting a quantile as a probability boundary is an essential concept (P0).* A 0.95 quantile is not “a value that occurs with 95% probability”; approximately 95% of the distribution lies at or below it under the declared convention.

== Empirical estimation and uncertainty

For observations stem:[X_1,\ldots,X_n], the empirical CDF is

[stem]
++++
\widehat F_n(x)=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf 1\{X_i\leq x\}.
++++

The empirical strict exceedance rate at stem:[u] is

[stem]
++++
\widehat p_u=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf 1\{X_i>u\}.
++++

If observations are independent and identically distributed, each indicator is Bernoulli with probability stem:[p_u], giving

[stem]
++++
\operatorname{SE}(\widehat p_u)
=\sqrt{\frac{p_u(1-p_u)}{n}}
\approx
\sqrt{\frac{\widehat p_u(1-\widehat p_u)}{n}}.
++++

The approximation is unreliable when the number of observed exceedances is small. Exact or score-based binomial intervals are preferable to an unqualified symmetric interval near zero or one. Dependence, unequal exposure, censoring, and threshold selection require a design-specific analysis.

Under a smooth positive density stem:[f] near the population quantile stem:[q_p=Q(p)], an empirical quantile has the large-sample approximation

[stem]
++++
\operatorname{SE}(\widehat q_p)
\approx
\frac{\sqrt{p(1-p)}}{\sqrt n\,f(q_p)}.
++++

Tail quantiles are difficult because stem:[p(1-p)] and, especially, the density stem:[f(q_p)] in the denominator determine precision. A sparse tail, clustering, or distribution shift can dominate the nominal sampling calculation. *Extreme quantile estimation is a core concept (P1), not a reliable extrapolation from a small convenience sample.*

== Visual connection between density, CDF, quantile, and exceedance

The example uses a lognormal model for a positive, right-skewed duration. The parameter values are illustrative and do not validate a lognormal model for any real process.

.Quantile and exceedance probability for a positive skewed distribution
[#quantile-exceedance-figure]
[%dynamic%open,python,fail-on-error=,output=matplotlib,figure-format=svg,figure-alt="Two labelled panels show a lognormal density with its upper tail shaded beyond a threshold and a cumulative distribution with a marked 90 percent quantile",figure-caption="A threshold maps to a tail area, whereas a probability level maps through the inverse CDF to a quantile. The same distribution underlies both views.",result-interpretation="The shaded density area answers the exceedance question for a fixed eight-hour limit. The marked cumulative-distribution intersection answers the inverse question for a fixed probability level of 0.90."]
----
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

# Declare one positive, skewed reference model using SciPy's shape and scale convention.
shape = 0.55
scale = 4.0
duration_model = stats.lognorm(s=shape, scale=scale)
x = np.linspace(0.01, 14.0, 700)

# Map a probability level to a quantile and an operational limit to a tail probability.
probability_level = 0.90
q90 = duration_model.ppf(probability_level)
limit = 8.0
exceedance_probability = duration_model.sf(limit)
print(f"90% quantile: {q90:.2f} hours")
print(f"P(duration > {limit:.1f} hours): {exceedance_probability:.3f}")

# Show the tail area in density space and the quantile in cumulative-probability space.
fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(10.5, 4.0), constrained_layout=True)
axes[0].plot(x, duration_model.pdf(x), color="#1f4e79", linestyle="-", label="density")
tail = x > limit
axes[0].fill_between(x[tail], duration_model.pdf(x[tail]), color="#b22222", alpha=0.35)
axes[0].axvline(limit, color="#b22222", linestyle="--", label="8-hour limit")
axes[0].set(title="Exceedance is a tail area", xlabel="duration (hours)", ylabel="probability density")
axes[0].legend()

axes[1].plot(x, duration_model.cdf(x), color="#1f4e79", linestyle="-", label="CDF")
axes[1].axhline(probability_level, color="#444444", linestyle=":", label="probability 0.90")
axes[1].axvline(q90, color="#b22222", linestyle="--", label="90% quantile")
axes[1].plot([q90], [probability_level], marker="o", color="black")
axes[1].set(title="A quantile inverts the CDF", xlabel="duration (hours)", ylabel="cumulative probability")
axes[1].legend()

plt.show()
----

Changing the threshold changes the tail probability. Changing the probability level changes the quantile. These are inverse questions, not interchangeable inputs.

== Three different intervals and thresholds

Prediction interval:: A range intended to contain a future observation with a declared probability under the model. It describes outcome variation plus relevant predictive uncertainty.
Confidence interval:: A procedure for estimating an unknown parameter or estimand; its repeated-sampling coverage concerns the procedure, not the probability that a fixed parameter moves after the data are observed.
Central quantile interval:: The population range stem:[[Q(\alpha/2),Q(1-\alpha/2)]]. For a continuous distribution it contains probability stem:[1-\alpha]; point masses can make the contained probability larger. It describes distributional spread and is not automatically a prediction interval for a fitted model.

[IMPORTANT]
====
An *outcome threshold* stem:[u] defines an event such as stem:[X>u]. A *classifier decision threshold* converts a model score into an action and is selected from error costs, capacity, and validation evidence. Classifier thresholds are developed in xref:foundations/metrics-errors.adoc[Metrics, thresholds, and error analysis]. Do not use the two meanings interchangeably.
====

== Worked example: an empirical service-limit exceedance

[.worked-example-context#worked-exceedance-context]
=== Context

*Importance:* Essential (P0)

Among stem:[n=600] protected transactions, 18 exceed a service limit fixed in advance at stem:[u=8] seconds. Estimate the exceedance probability stem:[p_8=P(X>8)] and its finite-sample uncertainty. The first calculation treats transactions as independent and representative; the interpretation will revisit those assumptions.

[.worked-example-method#worked-exceedance-method]
=== Method

*Importance:* Core (P1)

The indicator stem:[I_i=\mathbf 1\{X_i>8\}] converts the threshold question into a Bernoulli mean. Therefore

[stem]
++++
\widehat p_8=\frac{18}{600}=0.030,
\qquad
\widehat{\operatorname{SE}}(\widehat p_8)
=\sqrt{\frac{0.030(0.970)}{600}}
\approx0.0070.
++++

The symmetric large-sample interval is

[stem]
++++
0.030\pm1.96(0.0070)
=0.030\pm0.0137
\approx[0.016,0.044].
++++

.Mathematical insight — exceedance estimation is Bernoulli estimation
[IMPORTANT]
====
[.notebook-insight-label]
*Mathematical insight.*

*Core (P1).* Once the service limit is fixed independently of these data, each observation contributes the indicator stem:[I_i]. The empirical exceedance rate is therefore a sample mean with variance approximately stem:[p(1-p)/n] under independence. Choosing the threshold after seeing the same sample changes the problem and invalidates this simple uncertainty calculation.
====

[.worked-example-implementation#worked-exceedance-implementation]
=== Implementation

*Importance:* Essential (P0)

The executable check derives every reported quantity from the declared threshold, count, and denominator and records the units explicitly.

[#exceedance-worked-table]
[%dynamic%open,python,fail-on-error=,output=table,table-caption="Service-limit exceedance calculation",table-description="The table exposes the threshold, protected sample size, exceedance count, empirical exceedance rate, plug-in standard error, and rough symmetric 95 percent limits."]
----
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd

# Compute every reported quantity from the declared count and denominator.
threshold_seconds = 8.0
sample_size = 600
exceedances = 18
rate = exceedances / sample_size
standard_error = np.sqrt(rate * (1.0 - rate) / sample_size)
lower, upper = rate + np.array([-1.0, 1.0]) * 1.96 * standard_error
assert np.isclose(rate, 0.03)

pd.DataFrame(
    {
        "value": [threshold_seconds, sample_size, exceedances, rate, standard_error, lower, upper],
        "unit": ["seconds", "transactions", "transactions", "proportion", "proportion", "proportion", "proportion"],
    },
    index=["threshold", "sample size", "exceedances", "rate", "standard error", "95% lower", "95% upper"],
).round(4)
----

[.worked-example-results#worked-exceedance-results]
=== Results

*Importance:* Essential (P0)

The empirical exceedance rate is stem:[18/600=0.030], with plug-in standard error approximately stem:[0.0070]. The rough symmetric 95% interval is stem:[[0.016,0.044]], so the finite protected sample does not support reporting the rate as an exact stem:[3\%].

[.worked-example-conclusion#worked-exceedance-conclusion]
=== Conclusion

*Importance:* Essential (P0)

About stem:[3\%] of the protected transactions exceeded the predeclared eight-second limit, with substantial finite-sample uncertainty relative to that rate. A score or exact binomial interval is preferable for production reporting. A traffic surge, repeated customer sessions, population shift, or choosing the threshold after inspecting these same observations invalidates the simple independent-binomial interpretation.

== Error ledger

[cols="2,3,3",options="header"]
|===
|Source |What can go wrong? |Evidence or response
|Measurement |Rounding, censoring, detection limits, or unit errors move observations across stem:[u]. |Audit units and resolution; perform threshold sensitivity analysis.
|Sampling |Few tail observations make stem:[\widehat p_u] and stem:[\widehat q_p] unstable. |Report counts and intervals; justify sample size from the tail question.
|Dependence |Bursts, repeated units, sites, or adjacent times reduce effective information. |Use cluster- or block-aware intervals and resampling.
|Threshold selection |Choosing stem:[u] after seeing the same data makes the reported probability selectively optimistic or pessimistic. |Predeclare the threshold or account for the selection procedure.
|Model approximation |A light-tailed family can severely understate rare events. |Compare empirical and fitted tails; challenge support and extrapolation.
|Population shift |The future operating regime may have a different tail. |Stratify by time and regime; define monitoring and revalidation conditions.
|Numerical error |Computing stem:[1-F(u)] can lose precision in an extreme tail. |Use a tested survival-function implementation.
|===
