A distribution is a mathematical model of possible outcomes and their probabilities. This lesson connects outcome type, support, probability mass or density, statistical moments, and a defensible choice of model family. The webpage and downloadable notebook are generated from this same AsciiDoc source.

== Learning outcomes

After this lesson, you should be able to:

. distinguish a probability mass, a density, a distribution function, and an empirical distribution;
. compute and interpret the first four standardized moment summaries when they exist;
. explain why a few moments do not determine operational tail risk;
. connect common distribution families to an outcome mechanism and identify a failed assumption;
. use numerical plots and diagnostics without treating a named family as established fact.

== Probability models and support

Let stem:[X:\Omega\to\mathcal X] be a random variable defined on a probability space, and let stem:[P_X] denote its distribution. The *support* stem:[\mathcal X] is the set of values that the model allows. Support is a first modelling constraint: a Gaussian model permits negative values, whereas counts and waiting times do not.

.Definition: Mass, density, and distribution function
[.def#def:mass-density-cdf]
****
For a discrete random variable, the probability mass function is

[stem]
++++
p_X(x)=\mathbb P(X=x),
\qquad
\sum_{x\in\mathcal X}p_X(x)=1.
++++

For an absolutely continuous random variable, a probability density stem:[f_X] satisfies

[stem]
++++
\mathbb P(a<X\leq b)=\int_a^b f_X(x)\,dx,
\qquad
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f_X(x)\,dx=1.
++++

In both cases, the cumulative distribution function is

[stem]
++++
F_X(x)=\mathbb P(X\leq x).
++++
****

A mass is a probability. A density value is not: only an area under the density is a probability, and a density can exceed one. The CDF is non-decreasing, right-continuous, and tends to zero and one at the two extremes. It also represents discrete, continuous, and mixed distributions in one notation.

*Distinguishing mass, density, and probability is an essential concept (P0).* Confusing a density height with an event probability produces incorrect scientific and operational conclusions.

== Moments as distribution summaries

.Definition: Raw and central moments
[.def#def:moments]
****
When the relevant expectations are finite, the stem:[k]-th raw and central moments are

[stem]
++++
m'_k=\mathbb E[X^k],
\qquad
\mu_k=\mathbb E\!\left[(X-\mathbb E[X])^k\right].
++++
****

[cols="1,2,2,3,1",options="header"]
|===
|Order |Population quantity |Name |What it can and cannot say |Importance

|1
|stem:[m'_1=\mu=\mathbb E(X)]
|Mean
|Expected level or long-run average under repeated sampling. It need not be a typical observed value for a skewed or multimodal distribution.
|Essential (P0)

|2
|stem:[\mu_2=\sigma^2]
|Variance
|Expected squared deviation from the mean. Standard deviation stem:[\sigma] restores the original unit but is still sensitive to extreme values.
|Essential (P0)

|3
|stem:[\gamma_1=\mu_3/\sigma^3]
|Skewness
|Standardized signed third moment. Its sign is often associated with the direction of asymmetry, but it does not by itself order two distributions by tail probability.
|Core (P1)

|4
|stem:[\gamma_2=\mu_4/\sigma^4-3]
|Excess kurtosis
|Standardized fourth moment relative to the Gaussian value. It measures fourth-power dispersion and is extremely outlier-sensitive; it is not simply “peakedness” or a complete measure of tail risk.
|Core (P1)
|===

For observed values stem:[x_1,\ldots,x_n], sample moments replace expectations with empirical averages. Higher sample moments are unstable because large observations are cubed or raised to the fourth power. Some population moments do not exist: a Student stem:[t_\nu] distribution has a finite mean only for stem:[\nu>1], finite variance only for stem:[\nu>2], and finite fourth moment only for stem:[\nu>4].

*Checking existence before interpreting a moment is a core concept (P1).* Software can return a finite sample variance or kurtosis even when the corresponding population moment is infinite.

Moments compress a distribution but normally do not identify it. Even equal means and variances do not imply equal asymmetry, quantiles, exceedance probabilities, or decision risk. These distributional quantities are developed in xref:foundations/quantiles-exceedance-risk.adoc[Quantiles, exceedance probabilities, and risk].

== Representative distribution families

The family name is not a conclusion. It is a hypothesis about support, data-generating mechanism, dependence, and shape that must be checked against the intended use.

[cols="2,2,2,3,1",options="header"]
|===
|Family |Type and support |Parameter convention here |Mechanism, use, and warning |Importance

|Bernoulli / binomial
|Discrete; stem:[\{0,1\}] or stem:[\{0,\ldots,m\}]
|Success probability stem:[p]; binomial trial count stem:[m]
|One binary trial or the number of successes in stem:[m] comparable independent trials. Dependence or varying stem:[p] invalidates the elementary binomial model.
|Essential (P0)

|Poisson
|Discrete counts; stem:[\{0,1,2,\ldots\}]
|Expected count stem:[\lambda] for a declared exposure; a rate model must include the exposure
|Counts from a constant-rate independent-event model. The equality stem:[\mathbb E(X)=\operatorname{Var}(X)=\lambda] is restrictive; overdispersion requires investigation.
|Core (P1)

|Gaussian
|Continuous; stem:[\mathbb R]
|Mean stem:[\mu], standard deviation stem:[\sigma>0]
|Symmetric, light-tailed measurement or approximation model. It conflicts with bounded support and may seriously understate tail risk.
|Essential (P0)

|Exponential / gamma
|Continuous; stem:[(0,\infty)]
|Exponential rate stem:[\lambda]; gamma shape stem:[k] and scale stem:[\theta]
|Waiting times and positive skewed quantities. Exponential memorylessness and gamma parameter conventions must be stated rather than assumed.
|Core (P1)

|Student stem:[t]
|Continuous; stem:[\mathbb R]
|Location, scale, and degrees of freedom stem:[\nu]
|Symmetric heavy-tailed variation. The scale is not the standard deviation, and low stem:[\nu] may make moments infinite.
|Core (P1)

|Empirical distribution
|Discrete mass stem:[1/n] on each observation, with repeated values accumulating mass
|Observed sample and optional design weights
|Descriptive distribution and resampling model. It represents no values beyond the observations without additional assumptions.
|Essential (P0)
|===

Other families become important when the support or mechanism requires them: categorical or multinomial outcomes, negative-binomial counts, beta proportions, and lognormal positive measurements. Learning a catalogue is less important than learning to justify support, parameterization, and assumptions.

.Probability distribution zoo

[%dynamic%open,python,fail-on-error=,output=matplotlib,figure-alt="Eight labelled panels show Bernoulli, binomial, Poisson, empirical, Gaussian, exponential, gamma, and Student t distributions; discrete distributions use bars and continuous distributions use curves",figure-caption="Representative probability mass functions, an empirical distribution, and probability densities. ----
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

# Build probability masses for three named discrete models and one empirical model.
empirical_values = np.array([0, 0, 1, 1, 1, 2, 2, 3, 4])
empirical_x, empirical_counts = np.unique(empirical_values, return_counts=True)
discrete_panels = [
    ("Bernoulli (p=0.35)", np.arange(2), stats.bernoulli.pmf(np.arange(2), 0.35)),
    ("Binomial (m=12, p=0.35)", np.arange(13), stats.binom.pmf(np.arange(13), 12, 0.35)),
    ("Poisson (rate=4)", np.arange(13), stats.poisson.pmf(np.arange(13), 4)),
    ("Empirical distribution", empirical_x, empirical_counts / empirical_counts.sum()),
]

# Evaluate continuous densities only on grids compatible with their support.
x_real = np.linspace(-4, 4, 500)
x_positive = np.linspace(0, 8, 500)
continuous_panels = [
    ("Gaussian (mean=0, sd=1)", x_real, stats.norm.pdf(x_real)),
    ("Exponential (rate=1)", x_positive, stats.expon.pdf(x_positive, scale=1)),
    ("Gamma (shape=2, scale=1)", x_positive, stats.gamma.pdf(x_positive, a=2, scale=1)),
    ("Student t (3 df)", x_real, stats.t.pdf(x_real, df=3)),
]

# Bars encode probability masses; curves encode densities whose areas equal one.
fig, axes = plt.subplots(2, 4, figsize=(13, 6.2), constrained_layout=True)
for ax, (title, x, probability) in zip(axes[0], discrete_panels, strict=True):
    ax.bar(x, probability, color="#4c78a8", edgecolor="black")
    ax.set(title=title, xlabel="outcome", ylabel="probability mass")

for ax, (title, x, density) in zip(axes[1], continuous_panels, strict=True):
    ax.plot(x, density, color="#8b1e1e", linewidth=2, linestyle="-")
    ax.fill_between(x, density, color="#d9a6a6", alpha=0.35)
    ax.set(title=title, xlabel="value", ylabel="probability density")

plt.show()
----

Read the figure by checking support, discreteness, symmetry, skewness, and tail decay. Do not compare curve heights across panels as though they were probabilities.

== From mechanism to an adequate model

[cols="2,3,3,1",options="header"]
|===
|Question |Company example |Research example |Useful first check

|Is the outcome binary, a count, real-valued, bounded, or strictly positive?
|Conversion, defect count, latency, utilization proportion
|Clinical endpoint, detection count, concentration, bounded response
|Compare observed values and units with the proposed support.

|What mechanism could generate the observations?
|Arrivals per machine-hour, time between failures, multiplicative transaction values
|Event incidence, waiting time, additive measurement error
|State exposure, independence, stationarity, and aggregation assumptions.

|Which distributional features affect the decision?
|Capacity planning, warranty cost, fraud investigation, service reliability
|Precision, physical safety limits, reproducibility, rare-event frequency
|Compare centre, dispersion, asymmetry, and the relevant tail region.

|Does the fitted family fail visibly or numerically?
|Count variance far exceeds its mean; residuals retain seasonality
|Impossible support, multimodality, unexplained group structure
|Use stratified plots, residual diagnostics, and protected predictive checks.
|===

[TIP]
====
Start from the outcome and decision, not a favourite distribution. A good model is adequate for a declared purpose; it is not certified by a familiar bell-shaped histogram.
====

== Worked example: a failed Poisson implication

[.worked-example-context#worked-poisson-context]
=== Context

*Importance:* Essential (P0)

Weekly incident counts from comparable machines have sample mean stem:[\bar x=4.2] and sample variance stem:[s^2=11.5]. Assess the simplest constant-rate Poisson model. If stem:[X\sim\operatorname{Poisson}(\lambda)], then stem:[\operatorname E(X)=\operatorname{Var}(X)=\lambda]; estimating stem:[\lambda] by stem:[\bar x] therefore predicts variance stem:[4.2].

[.worked-example-method#worked-poisson-method]
=== Method

*Importance:* Core (P1)

Compare the observed and model-implied variance through the dispersion ratio:

[stem]
++++
D=\frac{s^2}{\bar x}
=\frac{11.5}{4.2}
\approx 2.74,
\qquad
D_{\mathrm{Poisson}}=1.
++++

The observed variance is stem:[11.5-4.2=7.3] counts-squared above the plug-in Poisson implication and is about stem:[174\%] larger than that implication.

.Mathematical insight — a distribution implies linked moments
[IMPORTANT]
====
[.notebook-insight-label]
*Mathematical insight.*

*Core (P1).* A probability model is more than a curve-fitting label: it imposes testable relationships. The constant-rate Poisson identity stem:[\operatorname{Var}(X)=\operatorname E(X)] makes stem:[D=s^2/\bar x] a direct diagnostic. A large discrepancy falsifies this implication for the observed summaries, but it neither supplies a formal sampling test without stem:[n] nor identifies the cause.
====

[.worked-example-implementation#worked-poisson-implementation]
=== Implementation

*Importance:* Essential (P0)

The executable check keeps the observed summaries separate from the fitted model implications and verifies the hand-computed dispersion ratio.

[#poisson-diagnostic-table]
[%dynamic%open,python,fail-on-error=,output=table,table-caption="Observed moments and the fitted constant-rate Poisson implication",table-description="Rows compare sample mean, sample variance, and variance-to-mean ratio with values implied by a Poisson model whose rate is estimated by the sample mean."]
----
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd

# Keep observed summaries separate from model implications.
observed_mean = 4.2
observed_variance = 11.5
poisson_rate = observed_mean
diagnostic = pd.DataFrame(
    {
        "observed": [observed_mean, observed_variance, observed_variance / observed_mean],
        "fitted Poisson implication": [poisson_rate, poisson_rate, 1.0],
    },
    index=["mean", "variance", "variance / mean"],
)
assert np.isclose(diagnostic.loc["variance / mean", "observed"], 2.7380952381)
diagnostic.round(3)
----

[.worked-example-results#worked-poisson-results]
=== Results

*Importance:* Essential (P0)

The fitted constant-rate Poisson model implies variance stem:[4.2] and dispersion ratio stem:[1], whereas the observed variance is stem:[11.5] and the ratio is approximately stem:[2.74]. The stem:[7.3] counts-squared discrepancy is too large to dismiss as rounding.

[.worked-example-conclusion#worked-poisson-conclusion]
=== Conclusion

*Importance:* Essential (P0)

The observed moments are inconsistent with the simplest constant-rate Poisson implication. The discrepancy is descriptive rather than a formal test because the number of weeks is not given, and it does not identify a cause. Rate heterogeneity, omitted exposure, clustering, serial dependence, or data errors can all create overdispersion. A negative-binomial model is one candidate to investigate, not an automatic repair.
