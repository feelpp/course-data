A sample mean is a numerical summary of observed data and an estimator of a declared population quantity. This lesson derives its sampling variability, explains when Gaussian and Student stem:[t] approximations are justified, and separates sampling uncertainty from bias, dependence, and model error. Review xref:foundations/probability-distributions-moments.adoc[Probability distributions and statistical moments] first.

== Learning outcomes

After this lesson, you should be able to:

. distinguish an estimand, an estimate, an estimator, and its sampling distribution;
. derive the expectation and variance of a sample mean under explicit assumptions;
. use and interpret standard errors, the central limit theorem, and a Student stem:[t] confidence interval;
. explain why larger stem:[n] does not repair bias, dependence, or population mismatch;
. use simulation to investigate finite-sample approximation and Monte Carlo error.

== Estimand, estimator, and estimate

Let stem:[X_1,\ldots,X_n] be observations from a population distribution stem:[P]. The target, or *estimand*, is the population mean

[stem]
++++
\mu=\mathbb E_P[X],
++++

provided stem:[\mathbb E_P|X|<\infty]. The sample mean estimator and its realized estimate are written

[stem]
++++
\widehat\mu_n=\overline X_n=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^nX_i,
\qquad
\widehat\mu_n(\mathcal D_n)=\overline x_n.
++++

The estimator is random before observing the sample; the estimate is the resulting number. A *sampling distribution* is the distribution of stem:[\widehat\mu_n] over repeated samples generated by the declared sampling design. It is not the histogram of observations within one sample.

== Exact mean and variance under independent sampling

.Proposition: Mean and variance of the sample mean
[.prop#prop:sample-mean]
****
If stem:[X_1,\ldots,X_n] have common finite mean stem:[\mu], common finite variance stem:[\sigma^2], and are independent, then

[stem]
++++
\mathbb E(\overline X_n)=\mu,
\qquad
\operatorname{Var}(\overline X_n)=\frac{\sigma^2}{n}.
++++

Linearity of expectation gives

[stem]
++++
\mathbb E(\overline X_n)
=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\mathbb E(X_i)=\mu.
++++

Independence makes all cross-covariances zero, so

[stem]
++++
\operatorname{Var}(\overline X_n)
=\frac1{n^2}\sum_{i=1}^n\operatorname{Var}(X_i)
=\frac{\sigma^2}{n}.
++++
****

Unbiasedness means that the estimator is correct on average over the sampling design. It does not mean that the realized estimate equals stem:[\mu], nor does it repair a sample drawn from the wrong population.

Without independence, the exact variance contains covariance terms:

[stem]
++++
\operatorname{Var}(\overline X_n)
=\frac1{n^2}\sum_{i=1}^n\sum_{j=1}^n
\operatorname{Cov}(X_i,X_j).
++++

Positive within-machine, patient, site, or time correlation usually makes the independent-row formula too small. The observational row is not automatically the sampling unit.

== Standard error and the square-root law

The standard error is the standard deviation of an estimator's sampling distribution. Under the preceding independent common-variance model,

[stem]
++++
\operatorname{SE}(\overline X_n)=\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}.
++++

Because stem:[\sigma] is usually unknown, use the sample standard deviation

[stem]
++++
s^2=\frac1{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^n(X_i-\overline X_n)^2,
\qquad
\widehat{\operatorname{SE}}(\overline X_n)=\frac{s}{\sqrt n}.
++++

Doubling stem:[n] multiplies the standard error by stem:[1/\sqrt2]; halving it requires four times as many independent observations. *The square-root law is an essential concept (P0).* It supports sample-size planning, but it does not reduce systematic measurement error, selection bias, or an incorrect target population.

== Central limit theorem and finite-sample behaviour

.Theorem: Classical central limit theorem
[.thm#thm:clt]
****
If stem:[X_1,X_2,\ldots] are independent and identically distributed with mean stem:[\mu] and finite non-zero variance stem:[\sigma^2], then

[stem]
++++
\frac{\sqrt n(\overline X_n-\mu)}{\sigma}
\xrightarrow{\mathcal D}\mathcal N(0,1)
\qquad\text{as }n\to\infty.
++++
****

The theorem is asymptotic. It gives no universal sample-size threshold: convergence depends on skewness, tail behaviour, and dependence. A finite-variance Student stem:[t_3] population satisfies the theorem but can converge slowly because rare observations strongly affect the mean. A Cauchy population has no finite mean or variance, so this theorem and stem:[s/\sqrt n] do not apply.

The CLT concerns the standardized estimator, not the distribution of individual observations. A sample mean may be approximately Gaussian even when the population is skewed, but this does not make the observations Gaussian.

== Confidence intervals and their interpretation

If the observations are independent and identically distributed Gaussian variables with common unknown variance, then

[stem]
++++
T=\frac{\overline X_n-\mu}{s/\sqrt n}
\sim t_{n-1}.
++++

Consequently, a two-sided stem:[100(1-\alpha)\%] confidence interval is

[stem]
++++
\overline x_n
\;\pm\;
t_{n-1,\,1-\alpha/2}\frac{s}{\sqrt n}.
++++

Under non-Gaussian independent sampling this interval is often used as a large-sample approximation, with adequacy depending on the population and sample size. For dependent or unequal-probability samples, use a variance estimator or resampling design that matches the sampling unit.

[IMPORTANT]
====
After the data are observed, a frequentist confidence interval is fixed. A 95% confidence statement concerns the long-run coverage of the complete procedure under its assumptions; it is not a 95% probability that a fixed stem:[\mu] lies in this particular interval. It also does not cover measurement bias, population mismatch, or arbitrary future observations.
====

For future outcomes, prediction intervals and quantile intervals answer different questions; see xref:foundations/quantiles-exceedance-risk.adoc[Quantiles, exceedance probabilities, and risk].

== Deterministic numerical experiment

The simulation compares sample means from populations sharing stem:[\mu=10] and stem:[\sigma=4] but having different shapes. It uses two sample sizes to show both the stem:[1/\sqrt n] contraction and the non-uniform quality of the Gaussian approximation.

.Sampling distributions under different population shapes
[#sampling-distributions-figure]
[%dynamic%open,python,fail-on-error=,output=matplotlib,figure-alt="Six labelled histograms compare repeated sample means for sample sizes 10 and 100 from Gaussian, shifted exponential, and heavy-tailed Student t populations; every panel marks the common population mean and reports observations outside the shared plotting range",figure-caption="Sampling distributions from 4,000 repetitions. All populations have mean 10 and standard deviation 4, but finite-sample shape and convergence differ. Each panel reports any estimates outside the common horizontal range so that extreme values are not silently hidden.",result-interpretation="Increasing the sample size contracts every sampling distribution around the common mean, but skewness and heavy tails affect the finite-sample shape and the frequency of estimates outside the common plotting range."]
----
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
import numpy as np

# Fix the seed so that the webpage, notebook, and CI obtain the same simulation.
rng = np.random.default_rng(20260722)
population_mean = 10.0
population_sd = 4.0
repetitions = 4_000
sample_sizes = (10, 100)


# Generate one population sample with the requested shape, mean, and standard deviation.
def draw_population(name, size):
    if name == "Gaussian":
        return rng.normal(population_mean, population_sd, size=size)
    if name == "Shifted exponential":
        return population_mean + rng.exponential(population_sd, size=size) - population_sd
    if name == "Student t (3 df)":
        # A t distribution with 3 df has variance 3, hence the scale adjustment.
        return population_mean + (population_sd / np.sqrt(3)) * rng.standard_t(3, size=size)
    raise ValueError(f"unknown population: {name}")


# Compute one estimator per repeated sample and compare empirical with theoretical SE.
population_names = ("Gaussian", "Shifted exponential", "Student t (3 df)")
sample_means = {}
for sample_size in sample_sizes:
    for name in population_names:
        values = draw_population(name, size=(repetitions, sample_size))
        sample_means[(sample_size, name)] = values.mean(axis=1)

    theoretical_se = population_sd / np.sqrt(sample_size)
    print(f"n={sample_size}, theoretical SE={theoretical_se:.3f}")
    for name in population_names:
        empirical_se = sample_means[(sample_size, name)].std(ddof=1)
        print(f"  {name}: empirical SE={empirical_se:.3f}")

# Use panel labels, edges, and a dashed reference so meaning does not rely on colour.
fig, axes = plt.subplots(2, 3, figsize=(12.5, 7.0), sharex=True, constrained_layout=True)
common_bins = np.linspace(2.0, 18.0, 49)
for row, sample_size in enumerate(sample_sizes):
    for column, name in enumerate(population_names):
        ax = axes[row, column]
        means = sample_means[(sample_size, name)]
        outside = np.count_nonzero((means < common_bins[0]) | (means > common_bins[-1]))
        ax.hist(
            means,
            bins=common_bins,
            color="#4c78a8",
            edgecolor="black",
            alpha=0.8,
        )
        ax.text(
            0.02,
            0.95,
            f"outside range: {outside}",
            transform=ax.transAxes,
            va="top",
        )
        ax.axvline(
            population_mean,
            color="#b22222",
            linestyle="--",
            linewidth=2,
            label="population mean",
        )
        ax.set(title=f"{name}, n={sample_size}", xlabel="sample mean (measurement units)")
        ax.legend()
    axes[row, 0].set_ylabel("number of repetitions")

plt.show()
----

The empirical standard errors should be close to stem:[4/\sqrt n]. At stem:[n=10], exponential skewness and occasional Student stem:[t_3] observations remain visible. At stem:[n=100], the central region is more concentrated and usually more Gaussian-looking, but finite simulation and heavy-tail variation remain.

Agreement with the theoretical standard error checks the code under the simulated model. It does not prove that observations in a company or research dataset are independent, unbiased, identically distributed, or representative.

== Worked example: interval for a mean

[.worked-example-context#worked-mean-uncertainty-context]
=== Context

*Importance:* Essential (P0)

Suppose stem:[n=25] independent calibration measurements give sample mean stem:[\overline x=12.0] units and sample standard deviation stem:[s=5.0] units. Estimate the population mean and construct a 95% Student interval, assuming the 25 observations are independent measurements from a population for which a mean and finite variance are meaningful.

[.worked-example-method#worked-mean-uncertainty-method]
=== Method

*Importance:* Core (P1)

First convert observation-to-observation spread into uncertainty of the mean:

[stem]
++++
\frac{s}{\sqrt n}=\frac5{\sqrt{25}}=1.0\text{ unit}.
++++

Using stem:[t_{24,0.975}\approx2.064], the 95% interval is

[stem]
++++
12.0\pm2.064(1.0)=[9.936,14.064]\text{ units}.
++++

The interval half-width is stem:[2.064] units; the full width is stem:[4.128] units.

.Mathematical insight — Studentisation accounts for estimating scale
[IMPORTANT]
====
[.notebook-insight-label]
*Mathematical insight.*

*Core (P1).* Replacing the unknown population standard deviation by stem:[s] adds uncertainty. Under independent Gaussian sampling, the standardised statistic stem:[(\bar X-\mu)/(S/\sqrt n)] has a Student stem:[t_{n-1}] distribution, so stem:[t_{24,0.975}=2.064] replaces stem:[1.96]. The theorem does not correct dependence, bias, or population mismatch.
====

[.worked-example-implementation#worked-mean-uncertainty-implementation]
=== Implementation

*Importance:* Essential (P0)

The executable check computes every interval component from named quantities and asserts the hand-calculated standard error.

[#mean-interval-worked-table]
[%dynamic%open,python,fail-on-error=,output=table,table-caption="Student interval for the calibration mean",table-description="Rows expose the sample size, sample mean, sample standard deviation, standard error, Student critical value, interval half-width, and lower and upper limits."]
----
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd

# Reproduce the hand calculation from named quantities.
sample_size = 25
sample_mean = 12.0
sample_sd = 5.0
t_critical = 2.064
standard_error = sample_sd / np.sqrt(sample_size)
half_width = t_critical * standard_error
lower, upper = sample_mean - half_width, sample_mean + half_width
assert np.isclose(standard_error, 1.0)

pd.DataFrame(
    {"value": [sample_size, sample_mean, sample_sd, standard_error, t_critical, half_width, lower, upper]},
    index=["n", "sample mean", "sample SD", "standard error", "t(24, 0.975)", "half-width", "95% lower", "95% upper"],
).round(3)
----

[.worked-example-results#worked-mean-uncertainty-results]
=== Results

*Importance:* Essential (P0)

The point estimate is stem:[12.0] units, its estimated standard error is stem:[1.0] unit, and the 95% Student interval is stem:[[9.936,14.064]] units. The stem:[4.128]-unit width is an immediate measure of the precision delivered by this sample and model.

[.worked-example-conclusion#worked-mean-uncertainty-conclusion]
=== Conclusion

*Importance:* Essential (P0)

The data support a mean estimate of stem:[12.0] units with the stated Student interval under the declared independent-sampling model. The interval quantifies repeated-sample uncertainty for the procedure; it is not a probability that this fixed interval contains a fixed parameter. If the 25 rows are repeated readings from five devices, treating them as 25 independent units can substantially understate uncertainty. Calibration bias and target-population mismatch are not included in the interval.

== Error ledger

[cols="2,3,3",options="header"]
|===
|Source |What the interval or simulation covers |What still requires investigation
|Sampling variation |Variation of the estimator under the declared sampling model |Sampling unit, representativeness, unequal inclusion, and dependence
|Measurement and selection bias |Not covered by stem:[s/\sqrt n] |Calibration, missingness, coverage, and sensitivity analysis
|Distributional approximation |CLT or Gaussian-model approximation to the estimator distribution |Skewness, heavy tails, influential observations, and small stem:[n]
|Variance estimation |Plug-in estimate stem:[s^2] under the stated design |Cluster-, block-, time-, or weight-aware variance estimators
|Monte Carlo approximation |Empirical behaviour over a finite number of simulated repetitions |Independent seeds and Monte Carlo standard error
|Floating-point computation |Negligible for the displayed scale in double precision |Large offsets, extreme weights, or cancellation require stability checks
|Population shift |Not covered by a same-population confidence calculation |Time-, site-, and regime-specific validation
|===
