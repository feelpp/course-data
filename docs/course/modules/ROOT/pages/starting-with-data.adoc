*Why do we have this course?* Because scientific results and many decisions in society depend on how data are produced, organised and transformed. We will learn to turn data into results whose meaning, limits and computation we can explain and check.

Start here with the meaning, forms and challenges of data. Then explore xref:csmi-connections.adoc[the place of this course in CSMI] and xref:data-in-action.adoc[applications and M1 internships]. You can follow this introduction with paper and a pen.

== 1. What are data, and what do they look like?

Data are recorded representations of observations, descriptions or computed results: information encoded so that we can store, share and process it. They can come from a sensor, a questionnaire, an image, a document or a simulation. Recording them involves choices: what to observe, when, with what instrument or model, and at what precision.

.Different origins, different representations, a shared need for context
image::introduction/data-origins.svg["Sensors produce readings and time series; cameras and microscopes produce pixel arrays; code and simulations produce computed tables and fields; people and digital activities produce text and records. CSV, Parquet, PNG, TIFF, HDF5, VTK and JSON are examples of formats. Units, time, source and acquisition or model settings are needed to interpret them.",width=1100]

Write just *22* on a sheet. Is it a temperature, an age, a count or an identifier? Add *22 °C, sensor A, room R, 7 September 2026 at 10:00 local time*. We can now interpret the number as a temperature reading in a stated context.

* The *value* is 22; the *variable* is temperature, expressed in °C.
* The *observation* is one sensor reading at a given place and time.
* The *metadata* describe how to interpret it: units, sensor location, time convention and acquisition method.
* A *dataset* collects related observations. It should retain the context needed to use them.

An observation can have several variables. A sensor identifier is a label even if it contains digits. A missing reading is a lack of information, not a temperature of zero.

[%collapsible]
.Reading common data representations
====
[cols="2,3,3",options="header"]
|===
|Form |What we see |What gives it meaning?
|Table |Rows and columns in a spreadsheet, CSV file or database |What one row represents; column types and units; identifiers.
|Time series or signal |Values indexed by time |Timestamps, sampling intervals and gaps.
|Image |A grid of pixel values, possibly with several channels |Pixel size, channel meaning and acquisition settings.
|Text or structured record |A report, a log entry or a JSON document |Language, field definitions and source context.
|Simulation output |An array or a field on a mesh |Coordinates, physical units, model parameters and numerical settings.
|===
====

These forms can overlap: a time series can be stored in a table. A file format tells us how values are encoded; it does not, by itself, tell us what they mean. Simulation outputs are data too, with assumptions and numerical errors to examine.

*Quick discussion:* name one dataset you have already encountered. What does one observation represent? Which piece of context would be lost if you shared only its numbers?

== 2. Why is processing a critical scientific step?

Processing means reading and organising data, checking their quality, converting representations or units, combining sources, and computing quantities that answer a question. Each transformation can preserve information, discard it or introduce an assumption.

=== Imperfect, massive, or scarce: different challenges

.Data volume and data quality are separate questions
image::introduction/data-challenges.svg["Three challenges: imperfect data have missing values, noise, errors, duplicates or bias; massive data require choices about memory, storage, movement and computation; scarce data require careful use of limited independent observations and explicit uncertainty. Many rows from one experiment do not create many independent experiments.",width=1100]

* *Imperfect data:* a missing measurement, an incorrect unit, sensor noise, a duplicated record or an unrepresentative sample require different responses. An unusual value may be a genuine event; inspect its source before deleting it.
* *Massive data:* long sensor streams, image sequences and simulation fields can exceed memory or make transfers expensive. Choose what to read, store, compress and compute, and check what a summary discards.
* *Scarce data:* a few expensive experiments or rare observed events limit what can be estimated and tested. Preserve information, examine uncertainty and avoid conclusions beyond the evidence.

These challenges coexist. A single experiment can generate millions of pixels while still providing only one independent experiment. More rows do not automatically remove bias or provide more independent evidence.

=== A small example: the calculation runs, but what does it mean?

Consider this *synthetic* table of readings from four sensors in a room at the same stated time:

[cols="1,2,1",options="header"]
|===
|Sensor |Recorded temperature |Unit
|A |22 |°C
|B |71.6 |°F
|C |20 |°C
|D |Missing |°C
|===

*Question:* what is the mean of the available temperature readings, expressed in °C? Before calculating, identify two things that need attention.

[%collapsible]
.A correct calculation needs a defined input
====
Convert B using `°C = (°F − 32) × 5/9`: 71.6 °F is 22 °C. The mean of the three available readings is therefore `(22 + 22 + 20) / 3 ≈ 21.3 °C`. Report that one of the four readings is missing.

Averaging the three raw numbers gives about 37.9, with no consistent temperature unit. Replacing the missing reading by zero after conversion gives 16 °C, but invents a measurement. Both computations can run without a software error.

The 21.3 °C result describes the available readings at that time. Calling it the temperature of the whole room, or of the whole day, would require further evidence about sensor locations, calibration and times. Retain the original values and record the conversion and missing-data rule so another person can check the result.
====

The same issue arises when a duplicated row receives extra weight, a date is misread, an image loses its physical scale, or two sensors use different clocks. A plot, model or decision inherits those choices. More computation cannot recover context that was discarded.

=== Why this matters for science and society

Digital instruments and software make data easier to produce, combine and reuse. Automated analyses can then carry the same processing choice into many downstream results and decisions. The need to check meaning, quality and limits grows with that reuse.

[cols="1,3,3",options="header"]
|===
|Context |What data make possible |Why processing matters
|Science |Test a hypothesis, compare a simulation with measurements, reuse an experiment |Preserve units, uncertainty and provenance so the comparison is meaningful and reproducible.
|Society |Inform energy planning, transport or the allocation of public resources |Check whose situations are represented and explain the evidence and limits behind a decision.
|People and organisations |Share observations and collaborate across teams |Control access to sensitive information and document transformations so results can be questioned and checked.
|===

For example, an energy estimate can inform a renovation choice; missing certain kinds of buildings can distort that estimate. The responsibility extends from reading a file to deciding what a result supports and who may be affected by its use.

Our working sequence is *question → data and context → checked transformations → analysis → conclusion with limits*. We revisit earlier steps when a check reveals a problem.

By the end of the course, you should be able to explain what a dataset represents, justify its transformations, evaluate an analysis against its question, and deliver a result someone else can reproduce. Python, tables, plots and models are the tools we will use to practise these skills.
