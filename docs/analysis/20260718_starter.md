## Executive recommendation

The archive is a valuable source collection, but it is not yet a coherent, reproducible course. It contains 37 Jupyter notebooks and one PDF (about 33.5 MB uncompressed). Its strongest material is the mathematical explanation of statistical learning, probabilistic modelling, regularisation, bias–variance, decision trees, ensembles, losses, automatic differentiation, and optimisation. The more recent JAX notebooks contain useful bridges toward Scientific Machine Learning.

However, the collection currently behaves more like a combined **introductory machine learning + neural networks + JAX** course than the CSMI S1 course described as **data cleaning, preprocessing, feature selection, clustering, classification, and efficient analysis of large datasets**. It under-covers the actual data-processing lifecycle and over-allocates attention to hand-built neural-network training. It also lacks a reproducible software environment, local and licensed data assets, systematic student/instructor variants, explicit learning outcomes, assessment rubrics, tests, and a publication structure.

The recommended direction is therefore:

1. **Keep and rewrite the mathematical spine.** The material on learning principles, probabilistic modelling, regularisation, bias–variance, trees, ensembles, classification losses, and metrics is highly appropriate for mathematically strong CSMI students.
2. **Build the missing data-processing spine.** Data acquisition, provenance, schema and type validation, missing and anomalous values, joins and reshaping, exploratory analysis, leakage-safe pipelines, feature construction/selection, PCA and clustering, columnar formats, memory/performance, dataset documentation, privacy, and reproducibility must become first-class material.
3. **Reduce JAX to a short optional bridge in this course.** JAX arrays, `grad`, `jit`, and `vmap` are useful for CSMI and downstream SciML, but the detailed PyTree/model/Optax/MLP sequence should move to *Scientific Machine Learning 1* or an optional extension track. JAX itself describes its core value as NumPy-like array computing with autodiff, JIT, vectorisation, and accelerator portability; those are the right bridge concepts, not a second deep-learning course inside 28 hours ([JAX quickstart](https://docs.jax.dev/en/latest/quickstart.html)).
4. **Use Antora and `feelpp/antora-ui`.** This is a good fit and should follow the successful structure of `course-project`, but with pinned versions, a stronger notebook pipeline, notebook execution in CI, and a public/private split for assessments.
5. **Make importance visible everywhere.** Every concept, page, notebook, exercise, and assessment objective should carry a priority: P0 essential, P1 core, P2 extension, or P3 deferred/advanced. P0 and P1 are taught and assessed; P2 is selective or project-based; P3 is linked or moved downstream.
6. **Use one scientific/industrial data story across the semester.** A stable, licensed, checksummed collection of sensor/simulation/operational tables should recur across wrangling, visualisation, modelling, performance, and reporting. Smaller contrasting datasets can support classification and clustering.
7. **Assess process as well as the final score.** Students should be evaluated on data audit, leakage prevention, baseline choice, validation, numerical correctness, reproducibility, tests, interpretation, limitations, and communication—not only model accuracy.

In short: **Antora is the right publication backbone; the archive is the right source of ideas; neither should be copied unchanged.** The new course should be designed as a reproducible, data-lifecycle course for applied mathematicians, with statistical learning as its analytical core and a controlled bridge to SciML.

## 1. Evidence and constraints

### 1.1 CSMI student profile

The CSMI intake is not a generic data-science audience. The programme admits M1 students primarily from a **Bachelor’s degree in mathematics or equivalent** and aims to train high-level mathematicians with a dual competence in mathematics and computer science ([CSMI overview](https://csmi.cemosis.fr/fr/csmi/overview.html)). The programme combines theory and applications and exposes students to industrial and research problems through projects and M1/M2 internships. Students are expected to develop depth in algorithms, scientific computing, PDEs, statistics, probability, algebra, and geometry, while mastering programming languages, software, and digital platforms.

This has several consequences for the course:

- mathematical derivations are an asset and should not be replaced by recipes;
- Python fluency will be heterogeneous even when mathematical maturity is high;
- the course must connect data methods to modelling, simulation, optimisation, HPC, signal/image processing, and SciML;
- students need professional engineering habits early because projects and internships follow quickly;
- examples should include scientific and industrial data, not only consumer ML benchmarks;
- an explicit diagnostic and a short Python/NumPy/pandas ramp are necessary, but this course should not become a basic programming course;
- all public course-site and student-facing written material must be in English. Legacy French material is rewritten before publication; it is not maintained as a parallel public site.

The official skill set includes problem solving, programming in C/C++/Python/Rust, analysis of large datasets, algorithm design, HPC, databases, machine learning, project management, communication, collaboration, research, and professional/ethical practice. The S1 course is explicitly associated with **Data Analysis Skills** and **Machine Learning** and its micro-skills are cleaning/preprocessing, feature selection, clustering/classification, and efficient large-data analysis ([CSMI 2024–2028 course and skills section](https://csmi.cemosis.fr/fr/csmi/csmi-2024-2028/index.html#_courses)).

### 1.2 Position in the programme

The course is scheduled in M1/S1 for 3 ECTS and 28 hours equivalent TD. It runs beside Operating Systems, Algorithmics & Graphs, Project 1, Database, C++, HPC 1, Scientific Computing 1/2, and Random Models. This adjacency should be used deliberately:

- **Project 1** owns Git/GitHub, CI/CD, containers, and project management as general tools; this course applies them to data workflows.
- **Database** owns relational design, SQL, and NoSQL in depth; this course uses query concepts and dataframe/SQL interoperability without duplicating database theory.
- **Random Models** owns probability and stochastic processes in depth; this course uses probability to justify losses, likelihood, sampling, and uncertainty.
- **Scientific Computing and Optimisation** own numerical methods and optimisation theory in depth; this course connects them to regression, conditioning, gradient methods, and computational cost.
- **SciML 1 in S2** is the natural destination for detailed autodiff, neural-network construction, Optax training loops, accelerators, and physics-informed methods.

This boundary is important. Without it, 28 hours will be consumed by neural-network mechanics while the students still lack dependable data-cleaning and validation skills.

### 1.3 Modern company and research needs

The proposed redesign is consistent with both the programme and current practice:

- AI and big data, technological literacy, analytical thinking, and continuous skill development remain prominent in the [World Economic Forum Future of Jobs Report 2025](https://reports.weforum.org/docs/WEF_Future_of_Jobs_Report_2025.pdf). This supports teaching adaptable concepts and sound workflows rather than a catalogue of APIs.
- Research data should be findable, accessible, interoperable, and reusable; the FAIR principles emphasise machine-actionable metadata and stewardship ([FAIR principles](https://doi.org/10.1038/sdata.2016.18)). Reproducible environments, provenance, licences, metadata, and checksums are therefore not optional research extras.
- Reliable ML requires lifecycle risk thinking. The NIST AI RMF organises this as **Govern, Map, Measure, Manage** ([NIST AI RMF Core](https://airc.nist.gov/airmf-resources/airmf/5-sec-core/)). At course scale this becomes: define the use and stakeholders, document the data, measure performance and uncertainty, analyse failure modes, and state limits.
- In France and the EU, students must know when data are personal, why purpose and minimisation matter, and why training data and models require security. The [CNIL recommendations for AI development](https://www.cnil.fr/fr/developpement-des-systemes-dia-les-recommandations-de-la-cnil-pour-respecter-le-rgpd) explicitly cover dataset construction, training, GDPR, security, and documentation. The EU AI Act’s AI-literacy provisions have applied since February 2025 ([European Commission AI literacy page](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/ai-talent-skills-and-literacy)). This course needs operational literacy, not a law lecture.
- Documentation is part of technical quality. [Datasheets for Datasets](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/datasheets-for-datasets/) and [Model Cards](https://research.google/pubs/model-cards-for-model-reporting/) offer practical templates for intended use, provenance, composition, limitations, and subgroup performance.
- Leakage-safe preprocessing is central. The scikit-learn documentation recommends splitting before learned preprocessing and using pipelines so transformations are learned only from training data ([scikit-learn common pitfalls](https://scikit-learn.org/stable/common_pitfalls.html)). This is currently not systematic enough in the archive and should become a P0 assessed skill.
- The existing [CSMI AI policy](https://csmi.cemosis.fr/fr/csmi/ai-policy.html) already requires disclosure, source verification, a reproducibility log, protection of confidential/personal/exam data, and student responsibility. Course repositories and assessments should implement this policy directly.

## 2. Archive inventory and technical audit

### 2.1 What is present

The archive contains:

- **37 notebooks**, all nbformat 4.0;
- **one 28-page PDF**, *Lecture 03: Regression with Kernels* (Purdue, 2020);
- about **33.5 MB** of files;
- six current/top-level teaching notebooks, twelve JAX notebooks, six legacy teaching notebooks, twelve exam/correction notebooks, one one-cell programme notebook, and the PDF;
- Python ecosystems spanning NumPy, pandas, matplotlib, SciPy, scikit-learn, statsmodels, CVXPY, Graphviz, TensorFlow/Keras, JAX, Optax, Hyperopt, geopandas, folium, seaborn, and SymPy.

There is no README, licence manifest, dependency specification, lock file, local dataset bundle, data registry, automated test suite, build system, learning-outcome map, rubric, or explicit separation of public/student/instructor material.

All 37 notebooks carry Colab metadata, but only 20 declare Python in language metadata. No notebook uses cell tags. Stored execution state is inconsistent: many cells have outputs while execution counts are null. One legacy pandas notebook preserves an `AttributeError` output. Several notebooks intentionally contain incomplete cells or interactive IPython syntax, which means simple static parsing reports errors; these blanks are pedagogically legitimate, but without tags there is no machine-readable distinction between an exercise, solution, demo, and accidental breakage.

Output and embedded-image bloat is substantial. Examples include:

- `ancien/variantes d_architectures de réseaux de neurones.ipynb`: 10.1 MB;
- `avec jax/90-PROF = les classifications multiclasses.ipynb`: 2.9 MB;
- `avec jax/100-PROF = rappel = études 2d.ipynb`: 2.4 MB;
- `exam_anciens/80=frontière de decission.ipynb`: 2.3 MB;
- `avec jax/20-PROF = descente de gradient et Adam.ipynb`: 1.8 MB.

This will make reviews and Git history noisy. Outputs and binary images should be stripped from canonical notebooks, with selected figures regenerated in CI and stored as named image assets.

### 2.2 Content strengths

The best reusable ideas are:

- a mathematical progression from random variables and unknown target functions to empirical loss, train/validation/test separation, regularisation, and model selection;
- clear connections between least squares and maximum likelihood;
- useful explanations of conditional distributions, cross-entropy, logits, softmax, and classification boundaries;
- hands-on bias–variance experiments;
- interpretable tree and ensemble examples;
- concrete implementation of tensor semantics, automatic differentiation, gradient descent, Adam, batching, and optimisation in JAX;
- practical error analysis through confusion matrices and inspection of misclassified samples;
- ablation questions in the 2025–2026 exam, which are more diagnostic of understanding than merely asking students to reproduce an API;
- an informal difficulty notation using hearts, which can evolve into the formal P0–P3 priority and difficulty metadata.

The authorial voice is often direct and engaging. The material repeatedly asks students to predict, code, compare, and explain. That should be retained while improving precision, structure, and reproducibility.

### 2.3 Content and engineering weaknesses

The following issues prevent direct publication:

1. **Course-scope imbalance.** Dataframes, cleaning, schemas, provenance, clustering, dimensionality reduction, data validation, efficient formats, and large-data workflows are absent or trapped in legacy notebooks. JAX/MLP training dominates recent material.
2. **No coherent progression for students.** Filenames encode order but not prerequisites, outcomes, duration, assessed status, or student/instructor variants.
3. **Framework churn.** The archive mixes old TensorFlow/Keras code with JAX and current scikit-learn. TensorFlow is sometimes imported only to download MNIST. Older APIs and examples will be brittle.
4. **External runtime dependencies.** Several notebooks clone `vincentvigon/assets_ml` or `assets_neurone`, download remote archives, call Keras dataset loaders, or embed GitHub/Google Drive images. A network change can break a class or exam.
5. **Unknown or incomplete licensing.** Some material cites or adapts *Hands-On Machine Learning*. The kernel PDF states “All Rights Reserved”; it should not be redistributed without permission. Remote datasets and images need per-asset licences and attribution.
6. **Notebook state and size.** Large stored outputs and base64 images obscure source diffs. Canonical notebooks are not clean-room executable artifacts.
7. **Solutions are mixed with prompts.** Custom markers such as `ººuntil`, `#@title Solution`, hidden booleans, and `PROF` filenames are not a dependable release mechanism.
8. **Numerical practice needs updating.** The linear-regression notes explicitly form `(X^T X)^{-1}X^T y`. This is useful algebraically, but implementation should teach `lstsq`, QR, SVD, conditioning, and regularisation rather than explicit inversion.
9. **Terminology and correctness need editorial review.** There are frequent spelling mistakes, unfinished drawings, informal statements that need qualification, and occasional code typos (for example `jnp.jax.value_and_grad` in the 2025–2026 exam prompt).
10. **No testing or performance contract.** There is no “Restart & Run All” guarantee, time/memory budget, expected metric tolerance, deterministic seed policy, or CPU-only CI subset.
11. **Assessment operations are fragile.** Some exams instruct students to copy a Colab notebook and share it with a personal Gmail address. The latest exam depends on another notebook by classroom number, uses hardware-dependent ten-second training runs, and has no explicit point-level rubric in the distributed notebook.
12. **Privacy and integrity are not enforced by the artifacts.** The CSMI AI policy is strong, but notebooks do not yet include AI-use declarations, dataset documentation, or confidential-data warnings.

## 3. Priority model

### 3.1 Definitions

Every course artifact should show one of these labels.

| Priority | Meaning | Teaching and assessment contract |
|---|---|---|
| **P0 — Essential** | Every CSMI student must be able to use and explain it safely by the end of S1. | Taught explicitly, practised more than once, assessed individually, eligible for the final exam. Failure on a safety-critical P0 item such as leakage may cap the corresponding assessment criterion. |
| **P1 — Core** | Important professional/scientific competence that forms the analytical depth of the course. | Taught and practised, assessed in at least one control/project or exam; students may use documented library implementations after explaining assumptions. |
| **P2 — Extension** | Valuable bridge to a domain, tool, or downstream CSMI course; not all students need equal implementation depth. | Demonstrated or offered as guided extension; assessed only when announced, usually in a project choice or bonus section. |
| **P3 — Advanced/deferred** | Useful but outside the 28-hour contract, too framework-specific, or owned by another course. | Linked as enrichment or moved to SciML/HPC/Database/Project; never silently required in an S1 exam. |

Priority is not the same as difficulty. Each artifact should also declare difficulty (`D1 guided`, `D2 independent`, `D3 synthesis`) and expected time. A concept can be P0-D1 (missing-value audit) or P0-D3 (designing a leakage-free evaluation).

### 3.2 Full concept/method/technique priority map

| Concept, method, or technique | Priority | Required depth in this course | Assessment status and reason |
|---|---:|---|---|
| Problem formulation; observation, feature, target, unit of analysis | P0 | Define a data question and the prediction/inference/description goal before coding. | Individual; prevents technically correct answers to the wrong problem. |
| Data lifecycle: acquire → validate → transform → analyse/model → evaluate → communicate → preserve | P0 | Explain and implement a small end-to-end workflow. | Individual and project; organising spine for industry and research. |
| Provenance, licence, citation, consent/purpose, personal/confidential data | P0 | Record source, owner, licence, retrieval/version, permitted use, and privacy constraints. | Mandatory checklist; aligns with FAIR, CNIL, and CSMI AI policy. |
| Reproducible environment, pinned dependencies, random seeds, deterministic rerun | P0 | Recreate the environment and run the pipeline from a clean checkout. | Automatic CI plus rubric; research/industry baseline. |
| Python/NumPy array fundamentals and vectorisation | P0 prerequisite | Use shapes, dtypes, broadcasting, masks, reductions, and vectorised operations; Python syntax is remedial material. | Short diagnostic and labs; foundation for pandas, sklearn, JAX, HPC. |
| pandas Series/DataFrame; indexes and column semantics | P0 | Create, inspect, select, filter, assign safely, and reason about copies. | Practical assessment. |
| Tidy data, long/wide form, reshape, group-by, aggregation | P0 | Transform tables while preserving unit of analysis. | Practical assessment; frequent company/research task. |

## 4. Proposed course design

### 4.1 Course promise

> By the end of the course, a CSMI student can take a documented scientific or industrial dataset from raw tables to a reproducible, leakage-safe and computationally reasoned analysis; build and compare defensible baseline models or clusters; quantify and interpret performance and uncertainty; and deliver tested code, data documentation, and a concise technical report.

### 4.2 Learning outcomes

On successful completion, students should be able to:

1. formulate a data question, identify the unit of analysis, target, stakeholders, constraints, and risks;
2. audit provenance, licences, privacy, schemas, types, units, missingness, duplicates, anomalies, and representativeness;
3. transform and combine tabular data with validated joins, reshaping, aggregation, and executable quality checks;
4. perform and communicate reproducible EDA without leaking information from evaluation data;
5. construct a scikit-learn pipeline for heterogeneous features and select appropriate baselines, splits, cross-validation, and metrics;
6. explain and apply linear/logistic models, likelihood and loss, Ridge/Lasso, bias–variance, trees, forests, boosting, PCA, and clustering at the priority depth above;
7. reason about time and memory, choose appropriate dtypes and storage, and use columnar/query techniques to avoid unnecessary materialisation;
8. test transformations and metrics with toy oracles and run the full analysis from a clean environment;
9. document datasets, experiments, assumptions, limits, subgroup/failure analysis, and AI assistance;
10. connect vectorised numerical/data workflows to downstream JAX/SciML and HPC topics without confusing a framework with the method.

### 4.3 Recommended 28-hour sequence

The table assumes fourteen two-hour blocks. It can be paired into seven four-hour sessions without changing the sequence. Each block should normally contain a brief concept segment, a guided example, an individual/pair task, and a written debrief.

| Block | Topic | Main priorities | Deliverable/checkpoint |
|---:|---|---|---|
| 1 | Course contract, data lifecycle, diagnostic, reproducible repository | P0 | Environment check, short diagnostic, source/licence record. |
| 2 | Dataframes, types, units, tidy data, reshape/group | P0 | Clean transformation notebook with assertions. |
| 3 | Joins, schemas, missing values, duplicates, anomalies | P0 | Data-quality report and tests. |
| 4 | EDA, sampling, representativeness, visual communication | P0 | One-page evidence-based EDA brief. |
| 5 | Splits, leakage, preprocessing pipelines, baselines | P0 | Leakage-safe mixed-feature pipeline. |
| 6 | Regression, stable least squares, likelihood, residuals | P1 | Analytical vs library result and conditioning experiment. |
| 7 | Logistic/softmax models, losses, metrics, thresholds | P0/P1 | Metric/threshold choice justified from a scenario. |
| 8 | Ridge/Lasso, bias–variance, CV, learning curves | P1 | Controlled experiment; **Control 1** may use this block or follow it. |
| 9 | Trees, forests, boosting, interpretation limits | P1 | Model comparison against baseline with error analysis. |
| 10 | PCA, k-means, hierarchical/DBSCAN, cluster validation | P1 | Unsupervised analysis with stability caveat. |
| 11 | Efficient processing: vectorisation, memory, Parquet, query pushdown | P1/P2 | Measured runtime/memory/storage comparison. |
| 12 | Reproducibility, FAIR/data cards, privacy, responsible AI, failure analysis | P0/P1 | Dataset datasheet, AI-use log, reproducibility manifest. |
| 13 | Integrated scientific/industrial mini-project clinic | P0/P1 | Pull request with tested pipeline and draft result card. |
| 14 | Synthesis, peer audit, short individual verification; optional JAX bridge demo | P0/P1 + P2 | **Control 2**/oral verification and course synthesis. |

If a full JAX lab is mandatory, place it after Block 11 and move some project clinic time outside contact hours. Do not remove cleaning, leakage, clustering, or reproducibility to make room for it.

### 4.4 Data strategy

Use a deliberately small portfolio:

1. **Semester spine dataset:** several related tables from an environmental sensor, industrial process, or Cemosis simulation workflow. It should contain timestamps, units, categorical metadata, missing/invalid values, join keys, and enough rows to motivate efficient storage and filtering.
2. **Classification dataset:** a licensed, moderate-size tabular problem with imbalance and meaningful error costs. Avoid making MNIST the default; students already understand images but learn little about messy joins and schemas from it.
3. **Unsupervised/scientific dataset:** simulation states, spectra, image-derived features, or measurements suitable for PCA and clustering.
4. **Synthetic oracle datasets:** generated in code for bias–variance, conditioning, leakage, and metric tests.

All datasets should be snapshots with:

- stable identifiers and checksums;
- source and retrieval date;
- licence and citation;
- schema, units, valid ranges, and missing-value conventions;
- a short datasheet;
- a script to fetch large originals, never a mandatory live download during class or an exam;
- a small CI sample and a documented full-data path;
- no personal data unless the pedagogical and legal basis is explicitly approved.

### 4.5 Notebook and exercise pattern

Every module should have:

- a concise Antora concept page;
- a 10–20 minute “predict before running” warm-up;
- a guided notebook with small deterministic cells;
- an independent exercise notebook with explicit acceptance criteria;
- reusable tests or hidden tests where appropriate;
- a short solution/explanation in the instructor build;
- one transfer question using a different dataset or split;
- time, priority, difficulty, prerequisites, downstream links, and assessed status.

Notebook cells should be tagged as `setup`, `demo`, `exercise`, `solution`, `test-public`, `test-hidden`, `slow`, `network`, or `instructor-only`. Canonical student notebooks must have outputs stripped. Published examples may include selected executed outputs produced by CI.

## 5. Assessment design

### 5.1 Recommended weighting

A balanced default is:

- **Control 1 — individual practical data audit and leakage-safe pipeline: 20%**
- **Mini-project — pair repository, result card, and short oral verification: 25%**
- **Control 2 — individual concepts/interpretation/reproducibility verification: 15%**
- **Final individual practical/written exam: 40%**

If programme rules require a stronger final exam, use 40% continuous assessment and 60% final while preserving the same tasks. The assessment blueprint should be approved before notebooks are written so that teaching, exercises, and exams stay aligned.

### 5.2 Control 1

Duration: 60–75 minutes, individual, frozen environment and dataset.

Students receive two small related tables and must:

- identify the unit of analysis and validate a join;
- detect type/unit/missing/duplicate/range problems;
- create two executable data checks;
- split correctly before learned preprocessing;
- construct a baseline pipeline;
- interpret one metric and one failure mode.

This directly tests P0 competence without requiring long training.

### 5.3 Mini-project

Pairs work in private GitHub Classroom repositories. Required artifacts:

- `README` with question and run instructions;
- data datasheet and licence/provenance record;
- reproducible environment lock;
- transformation/model code outside the notebook where reusable;
- tests and CI;
- notebook/report with baseline, validation, error analysis, uncertainty/limitations;
- result/model card;
- AI assistance log under the CSMI policy;
- a tagged release containing the final report and machine-readable metrics.

Each student receives a 5–8 minute individual oral/code verification. This reduces free-riding and validates understanding when AI assistants are allowed.

### 5.4 Control 2

Use a short individual audit of another analysis or a deliberately flawed pipeline. Students identify leakage, invalid metrics, join errors, irreproducibility, licensing/privacy problems, and unsupported conclusions. This tests professional judgement and is robust to API memorisation.

### 5.5 Final exam

Recommended duration: 2.5–3 hours, individual, CPU-only, offline-capable, provided environment. Combine:

- 25% data audit and pipeline repair;
- 25% mathematical/method reasoning (loss/likelihood, regularisation, bias–variance, PCA/clustering or trees);
- 25% implementation and tests on a small dataset;
- 15% evaluation/error/uncertainty interpretation;
- 10% reproducibility, provenance, privacy/licence, and limitations.

The dataset and context may change each year, but the blueprint and difficulty should remain stable. Publish a specimen exam and rubric early.

The 2025–2026 archive exam has two ideas worth preserving: **ablation experiments** and a **short individual verification quiz** after AI-assisted work. Improve them by:

- giving a self-contained repository rather than referring to “TP 160”;
- using fixed epoch/update budgets rather than ten seconds, which varies by hardware and compilation cache;
- assigning points and tolerance bands to every task;
- collecting through GitHub Classroom or the university LMS, not personal email;
- specifying the AI mode in the assessment header;
- capturing the exact commit and environment;
- requiring tests and a concise experiment table;
- keeping current exams and solutions in a separate private repository.

### 5.6 Common rubric

Use one rubric across controls, project, and exam:

| Criterion | Typical weight | P0 failure examples |
|---|---:|---|
| Problem and data understanding | 10% | Wrong unit of analysis; unsupported target definition. |
| Data quality and provenance | 15% | Unlicensed/unattributed data; invalid join; silent type/unit errors. |
| Pipeline and leakage control | 20% | Fitting preprocessing on all/test data; test-driven model selection. |
| Method choice and mathematical reasoning | 15% | No baseline; assumptions incompatible with the task. |
| Evaluation and uncertainty | 15% | Accuracy alone on imbalance; no failure analysis; test reused repeatedly. |
| Code quality, tests, and reproducibility | 15% | Cannot run from clean checkout; no oracle tests; hidden state. |
| Interpretation, limitations, communication, AI disclosure | 10% | Causal claims from association; missing AI declaration; confidential data sent externally. |

### 5.7 AI modes

Every assignment should declare one of three modes:

- **Mode A — prohibited external assistance:** controlled individual exam; only provided documentation.
- **Mode B — allowed with disclosure:** normal exercises/project; maintain `ai-notes/`, verify all output, cite primary sources, never upload confidential/exam/personal data.
- **Mode C — explicitly assessed AI collaboration:** students compare an AI suggestion with tests, documentation, and their own analysis; the verification trail is the learning objective.

This operationalises the existing CSMI policy instead of relying on a generic warning.

## 6. GitHub, Antora, and notebook architecture

### 6.1 Verdict on Antora and `feelpp/antora-ui`

Yes: use them. Antora provides a structured content model, component metadata, navigation, versioning, cross-references, attachments, and a playbook-driven build. Its playbook explicitly controls content sources, UI, extensions, output, and publication ([Antora playbook documentation](https://docs.antora.org/antora/latest/playbook/)); `antora.yml` cleanly identifies a component and its `modules` tree ([component descriptor documentation](https://docs.antora.org/antora/latest/component-version-descriptor/)).

The `course-project` repository demonstrates the right overall pattern:

- `site.yml` playbook;
- `docs/course/antora.yml` component;
- module-specific pages and `nav.adoc`;
- the released `feelpp/antora-ui` bundle and supplemental UI;
- `:page-jupyter:` AsciiDoc pages converted to notebooks;
- Antora Collector importing generated notebooks as attachments;
- GitHub Actions building and publishing `public/`.

The Antora Collector is specifically designed to run external generators and import their outputs into the content aggregate ([Collector documentation](https://docs.antora.org/collector-extension/latest/)). This is appropriate for generating student notebooks, executed examples, datasets, downloadable bundles, and assessment specimens.

The current latest visible `feelpp/antora-ui` release is v0.53 ([release page](https://github.com/feelpp/antora-ui/releases)). For reproducibility, pin a release asset in production rather than using `/releases/latest/` directly; update intentionally through a tested pull request.

- publish build logs and notebook execution reports as CI artifacts;

### 6.4 Public/private split

Use two repositories:

1. **Public `course-data`:** notes, student notebooks, exercises, public tests, datasets or fetch recipes, rubrics, specimen assessments, released past assessments after an agreed embargo, and the Antora site.
2. **Private `course-data-assessment`:** current controls/exams, hidden tests, solutions, grading scripts, moderation records, and variants.

A private branch inside a public repository is not a security boundary. Instructor solutions should be built as private CI artifacts or from the private assessment repository. Release automation can copy only allow-listed student files.

### 6.5 Source-of-truth strategy

Use a hybrid model:

- **Antora AsciiDoc is canonical** for concepts, guided labs, equations, navigation, and most exercises. The `course-project` `:page-jupyter:` approach can generate downloadable notebooks from `[source,python]` cells.
- **Notebook-native/Jupytext source is canonical only when needed** for rich exercise metadata, widget state, or nbgrader-like cells. Antora publishes a rendered explanation and attaches the generated student notebook.
- **Reusable logic lives in `src/course_data`, not hidden in notebook cell order.** Notebooks orchestrate, visualise, and explain; modules contain testable functions.
- **Solutions are transformations, not hand-maintained copies.** Tags/conditional blocks generate student and instructor variants.

This avoids both extremes: duplicating every narrative in `.adoc` and `.ipynb`, or making opaque binary JSON notebooks the only course source.

### 6.6 CI quality gates

Pull requests should fail when:

- an Antora page or xref is broken;
- a published concept lacks priority/outcome/time metadata;
- a student notebook contains `solution` or `instructor-only` cells;
- a canonical notebook contains outputs, execution counts, secrets, absolute paths, or undeclared network access;
- a fast notebook cannot restart and run on CPU within its time/memory budget;
- a dataset checksum/schema/licence record is missing;
- tests, linting, type checks, or data assertions fail;
- notebook figures or metric outputs drift outside reviewed tolerances;
- the release bundle contains the private assessment tree.

Scheduled/full CI can run slow notebooks and optional JAX accelerator checks. CPU correctness must remain the baseline; GPU availability should never determine whether a student can complete a P0 task.

## 10. Final judgement

The source archive is worth preserving and mining. Its mathematical explanations and experimental instinct match the CSMI profile well, and the newer JAX material can become an excellent bridge to SciML. But publication should be treated as a **curriculum redesign and software-product effort**, not a file-format migration.

The most important shift is from “a sequence of instructor notebooks about models” to “a tested learning system about the complete data lifecycle.” For CSMI, the differentiator should be the combination of:

- mathematical understanding;
- dependable data engineering at an appropriate S1 scale;
- numerical and computational reasoning;
- leakage-safe statistical learning;
- research-grade provenance and reproducibility;
- professional documentation, ethics, and communication;
- a clear path toward SciML and HPC.

Antora with `feelpp/antora-ui` is well suited to make that system navigable, versioned, and reusable. GitHub can make it testable and collaborative. The priority scheme makes the 28-hour contract honest: P0 and P1 form a rigorous complete course; P2 opens doors; P3 is intentionally handed to the right downstream course.

## 11. Post-implementation mathematical learning review (2026-07-18)

The first Antora implementation did not yet meet the intended standard for mathematically mature CSMI students. A source audit found no `stem` expression in any of the eight original foundation pages or seven analytical pages; the analytical pages were only 29--33 lines long. They specified laboratories and acceptance criteria, but did not provide enough definitions, derivations, assumptions, error analysis, worked examples, or exercises for a student to learn the method independently from the website. The concern was therefore substantive rather than cosmetic.

The corrective standard follows the pedagogical structure visible in the [Feel++ functional-analysis introduction](https://docs.feelpp.org/math/analyse-fonctionnelle/introduction.html): mathematical objects are defined, results and consequences are separated, notation is rendered with AsciiDoc `stem`, and examples and exercises are part of the chapter rather than delegated entirely to a notebook.

The revised course now includes a P0 chapter on mathematical background and error estimation. It establishes sample and population notation, empirical moments, vector norms, conditioning, expectation and conditional quantities, estimator bias, the bias--variance decomposition, empirical and population risk, excess-risk decomposition, sampling uncertainty under independence and dependence, predictive-performance uncertainty, and a complete error ledger. This chapter is deliberately a compact bridge, not a substitute for the CSMI probability, random-models, linear-algebra, or optimisation courses.

Mathematics is also embedded where it is used:

- foundation chapters formalise estimands, join multiplicity, weighted aggregation, missingness mechanisms, robust anomaly screens, empirical distributions and correlation, dependence-aware standard errors, leakage-safe transformations, paired baseline comparisons, classification/regression metrics, decision cost, numerical tolerances, and uncertainty records;
- all seven analytical chapters now contain notation, at least one definition, displayed objectives or transformations, mathematical properties or derivations, assumptions, error estimation, a worked example, reasoning exercises, and a notebook link;
- the content distinguishes data/measurement, approximation, estimation, selection, numerical/optimisation, stochastic, and distribution-shift errors instead of using “error” only as a prediction metric;
- automated repository checks reject an analytical chapter that loses its mathematical structure, and generated-site checks verify inline/displayed mathematics and MathJax loading.

The resulting division of labour is intentional: the web chapter should be sufficient for first learning and revision; the notebook should make claims executable and expose numerical behaviour; exercises should require hand reasoning and implementation; controls and exams should test transfer. External academic moderation and a pilot with representative students remain necessary to determine whether the level, pacing, and assumed prerequisites are correct in practice.
